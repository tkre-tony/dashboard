Hiap Hoe Tripled First-Half Profit To S$17.2 Million — But The Securities Portfolio Returned 103 Per Cent Of Group Pre-Tax Profit, And The Property Book Sits S$313.6 Million Below Its Own Valuers' Figure

Hiap Hoe's first half reads as a recovery: revenue up 5.3 per cent, profit after tax up 217 per cent to S$17.2 million. The segment note says where it came from. The Other Investments segment returned S$22.1 million against group profit before tax of…

By PropertyAtlas·18 August 2026·8 min read
Photo: Aloft Singapore Novena, West Wing hotel entrance, with the Hiap Hoe Building and Zhongshan Mall behind it. The integrated development at Zhongshan Park holds the group's flagship hotel alongside investment property carried at depreciated cost. Hotel income of S$44.4 million was 68 per cent of group revenue in the first half of 2026, while the Other Investments segment returned S$22.1 million against group profit before tax of S$21.4 million. Credit: Hiap Hoe Limited Annual Report 2025.

Hiap Hoe reported profit after tax of S$17.2 million for the first half of 2026 against S$5.4 million a year earlier, a rise of 217 per cent on revenue up 5.3 per cent. The segment note explains the distance between those two numbers, and it is not a property story.

The investment portfolio earned more than the company did

Hiap Hoe reports five operating segments. Four of them are businesses. The fifth is a portfolio of quoted and unquoted securities.

The Other Investments segment returned S$22.13 million. Group profit before tax was S$21.40 million. The portfolio therefore accounted for 103 per cent of the group's pre-tax result, and the three operating property and leisure segments together contributed S$11.33 million, a little over half as much (Note 4.1, page 13).

That is not a one-off. In the first half of 2025 the same segment returned S$11.53 million against group profit before tax of S$5.30 million, or 218 per cent. The securities book has out-earned the whole company in both halves. What changed this year is that the property side grew enough to be visible standing next to it, rising 98.3 per cent from S$5.71 million to S$11.33 million.

Ninety-five per cent of the increase was currency, interest and cheaper debt

Profit before tax rose S$16.10 million. Three lines account for almost all of it, and none of them is trading.

The currency swing is the single largest item and is attributed to movements in the Australian dollar, the euro and the United States dollar against the Singapore dollar (page 22). It is a translation and revaluation outcome rather than a realised trading result, and it reverses on the same mechanics that produced it. The prior period's S$3.95 million loss came from the same source.

The line most readers would expect to be doing the work went the other way. Fair value changes on other investments were S$9.27 million against S$11.96 million, down 22.4 per cent, a drag of S$2.68 million on the comparison.

Interest income of S$6.44 million is also a portfolio outcome rather than an operating one: S$6.32 million of it sits inside the Other Investments segment (Note 4.1, page 13), attributed to higher fixed income holdings and Australian dollar fixed deposits.

Fifty-five per cent of the portfolio is marked on unobservable inputs

The portfolio stood at S$521.33 million at 30 June, up 13.1 per cent from S$461.05 million at the end of December. It splits as follows (Note 5, page 16).

The Level 3 holdings invested directly by the group are valued on a comparable market approach using price-to-book multiples of peer companies at 1.77 times, unchanged from 31 December. The group discloses that a 5 per cent move in that multiple would move fair value by S$10.36 million (Note 6, page 17).

The reconciliation is worth reading against the headline, because the Level 3 book grew by purchase rather than by mark. It opened at S$255.38 million, took S$36.25 million of purchases, S$5.60 million of disposals and S$0.18 million of returned capital, and recognised S$0.71 million of fair value change. So the 55 per cent of the portfolio held on unobservable inputs produced 7.7 per cent of the S$9.27 million fair value gain. The gain came from the quoted side.

One name accounts for a large part of the unobservable half. The group holds 20.71 per cent of A2I Holdings S.A.R.L, a Luxembourg vehicle whose interest is in Essendi, formerly AccorInvest Group, which owns or leases hotels mainly operated by the Accor group. It is carried at S$103.94 million, down from S$105.99 million (Note 6, page 17). That single passive stake, in which the group states it has no significant influence, is 19.9 per cent of the entire portfolio and 36.3 per cent of the Level 3 book.

The direction of travel is clear in the cash flow. Cash and short-term deposits fell from S$140.10 million to S$109.45 million while purchases of other investments rose to S$59.54 million from S$23.42 million.

The property book sits S$313.6 million below its own valuers' figure

Hiap Hoe carries its investment properties, and its hotels, at cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment rather than at fair value. Among Singapore-listed property groups this is the minority choice, and it has a consequence that the interim statements do not restate.

An entity using the cost model must still disclose fair value. The 2025 annual report does, in three places.

The valuers' figure is 59.5 per cent above the balance sheet (Note 3.2(i), page 129; Note 13, page 138). The hierarchy note places the whole of it in Level 3, on income capitalisation and direct comparison (Note 37(d), page 173).

The surplus is S$313.59 million against S$744.02 million attributable to owners at that date. It is 42.1 per cent of the equity. Added back, net asset value per share moves from the reported 158.11 cents to roughly 224.8 cents.

It is also growing. The gap widened by S$15.29 million over 2025. Reported net asset value per share, meanwhile, has gone nowhere: 159.92 cents in 2021, 158.11 cents in 2025, down 1.1 per cent across five years (Financial Highlights, page 4). A portfolio that appreciated by S$313.59 million on its own valuers' numbers produced a flat net asset value line, and the income statement charged depreciation against it throughout: S$9.16 million in 2025 and S$4.67 million in the first half of 2026.

Two boundaries belong on that figure. First, S$840.71 million is a 31 December 2025 external valuation. At 30 June 2026 the group did not engage independent valuers; management carried out valuations taking the December methodology and assumptions forward (Note 13, page 20). There is no June surplus figure disclosed, and none is constructed here.

Second, property, plant and equipment of S$534.26 million, which holds Aloft Singapore Novena, Aloft Perth, Holiday Inn Express Trafford City, the Great Eastern Motor Lodge and the bowling centres, carries no fair value disclosure at all. The cost model for property, plant and equipment does not require one. The investment property gap is measurable. The hotel gap is not, and this article does not estimate it.

What did improve

Revenue rose 5.3 per cent to S$65.25 million. Hotel income of S$44.40 million was up 4.8 per cent and 68 per cent of the group total; rental income of S$15.72 million rose 10.1 per cent; the leisure business contributed S$5.13 million, down 3.9 per cent across the seven bowling centres. The group attributes both the rental and hotel increases to higher occupancies (page 22).

The segment results moved further than the revenue. Rental profit rose from S$1.55 million to S$4.83 million and hotel profit from S$3.10 million to S$5.62 million. Leisure fell from S$1.06 million to S$0.88 million. By geography, Singapore revenue was S$40.45 million, Australia S$20.32 million and the United Kingdom S$4.48 million.

Net asset value per share rose to 161.71 cents from 158.11 cents. Net current assets stood at S$381.67 million. Interest-bearing borrowings were essentially unchanged at S$859.39 million against S$853.52 million, and the group states it met all covenant requirements for the period (Note 14, page 21). An interim dividend of 0.25 Singapore cent has been declared, unchanged from last year; books close at 5pm on 24 August and it is payable on 3 September (page 24).

The case the other way

Cost accounting cuts in both directions, and the conservative side of it belongs in the record. Because Hiap Hoe never books revaluation gains, its reported profit has never been flattered by them either. Its property line moves only on impairment and reversal, which is a stricter test than annual marking: a S$4.08 million impairment on 130 Stirling Street in Perth in 2024, a S$2.54 million write-back in 2025 when the tenancy profile improved. A group that has taken its property write-downs through profit and loss has arguably earned the right to hold the upside off it.

The property operations also genuinely doubled, and that is the first half in some years where the operating businesses look like a recovery rather than a floor.

And the portfolio is disclosed strategy rather than drift. At the end of 2025, 45 per cent of it sat in real estate and hospitality by sector. The group has not stepped away from property; it has come to hold a large part of it in a second form, one that is marked to market every reporting date while the first form is not.

What the accounts do not resolve is which of the two the shareholder is being asked to own.
