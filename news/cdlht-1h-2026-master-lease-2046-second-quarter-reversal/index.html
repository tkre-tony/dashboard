CDL Hospitality Trusts Renewed Its Singapore Master Lease to 2046 Twelve Days Before Results — and the Second Quarter Inside Its Positive Half-Year Fell 3.1% on Revenue and 7.2% on Net Property Income

CDLHT's first half printed revenue up 1.4%, net property income up 1.8% and a distribution per stapled security up 8.6% — three positive lines. Two things underneath them are not in the summary. The master lease over Orchard Hotel, Grand Copthorne…

By PropertyAtlas·2 August 2026·11 min read
Photo: M Hotel, 81 Anson Road, Singapore — one of the four CDL Hospitality Trusts hotels whose master lease was renewed for a further twenty years to 18 July 2046, and now under a full renovation of all 415 rooms running to the third quarter of 2027. Credit: CDL Hospitality Trusts.

CDL Hospitality Trusts (CDLHT, SGX: J85) closed the six months to 30 June 2026 with revenue of S$126.9 million, up 1.4%, net property income of S$59.7 million, up 1.8%, and a distribution per stapled security of 2.15 cents, up 8.6%. Three positive lines, and a release that reads as a portfolio holding its footing through a difficult half for global travel.

Two things sit underneath that summary and are not in it.

The first is that the master lease over Orchard Hotel, Grand Copthorne Waterfront Hotel, M Hotel and Copthorne King’s Hotel — the arrangement that has underwritten the trust’s Singapore income since its 2006 listing — expired on 18 July 2026 and has been renewed for a further twenty years, to 18 July 2046. The renewal took effect twelve days before these results were released. It appears as a line in an appendix table and carries no commentary anywhere in the announcement. Four of six Singapore hotels sit under it, and Singapore is 62.0% of portfolio value.

The second is that the second quarter, taken on its own, went backwards on both revenue and net property income.

The quarter behind the half

CDLHT reports half-yearly. Its summary of results, its press release and its headline slides all present the six months. The quarterly split is disclosed — but only inside the geographic breakdown tables in Section 2 of the financial statements announcement, where the totals are printed without ever being carried up into a headline.

Revenue for the three months to 30 June 2026 was S$59.742 million against S$61.669 million a year earlier, a fall of 3.1%. Net property income was S$26.569 million against S$28.618 million, a fall of 7.2%.

Backing the second quarter out of the half leaves a first quarter of S$67.120 million of revenue and S$33.081 million of net property income — up 5.9% and 10.4% respectively on the same quarter of 2025, and matching to the decimal the figures PropertyAtlas reported in May. The first quarter carried the half. The second gave part of it back.

The trust’s own commentary is consistent with this without saying it in those terms: a strong opening quarter supported by the return of the Singapore Airshow in February, and a second quarter in which the Middle East conflict, higher jet fuel prices, elevated airfares and more cautious corporate travel policies produced cancellations and postponements. UN Tourism cut its 2026 forecast for growth in international arrivals by one to two percentage points from an initial 3% to 4% on the same conflict.

Singapore: the mall carried the market

Singapore portfolio net property income rose 0.8% for the half, or S$283,000, to S$33.72 million.

S$216,000 of that S$283,000 — 76% of it — came from Claymore Connect, the retail mall adjoining Orchard Hotel, on rental escalations already written into existing leases. Committed occupancy at the mall was 97.7% at 30 June.

The six Singapore hotels contributed the remaining S$67,000. Their net property income rose 0.2%.

RevPAR tells the same story in a different register. For the half, RevPAR rose 4.1% to S$172 — but average daily rate fell 0.7% to S$224, and the whole of the gain came from occupancy, up 3.5 percentage points to 76.7%. In the second quarter the pattern sharpens: RevPAR up 1.3% to S$160, occupancy up 1.7 percentage points to 73.1%, average daily rate down 1.0% to S$219. And second-quarter Singapore hotel net property income fell 6.3%, from S$14.151 million to S$13.261 million.

Occupancy bought at the expense of rate is a defensible trade in a soft quarter. It is not the same thing as pricing power, and it does not survive contact with a rising cost base.

The demand backdrop is thinner than the full-year forecast suggests. International visitor arrivals to Singapore were 8.2 million for the half, down 1.7% year on year and 87.8% of pre-pandemic levels, with weaker Indonesian arrivals the main drag. The Singapore Tourism Board’s full-year forecast of 17.0 to 18.0 million against 16.9 million in 2025 implies second-half arrivals between 2.8% and 14.5% above the second half of last year — after a first half that fell.

The distribution increase is a financing outcome

Net property income added S$1.055 million year on year. The distribution added S$2.426 million.

The gap is interest. Interest paid or payable to banks fell from S$24.141 million to S$16.445 million, a reduction of S$7.696 million or 31.9%. That single saving is 3.2 times the increase in the distribution; the improvement in property earnings covers less than half of it.

The saving was bought. In November 2025 the trust issued S$150.0 million of perpetual securities at 3.70% per annum, and in February 2026 a further S$100.0 million at 4.00%, both under a S$1.5 billion multi-currency debt issuance programme launched on 5 November 2025. The proceeds repaid bank borrowings. Blended, the S$250.0 million costs 3.82% a year — against a weighted average cost of debt of 2.8% on the borrowings that remain.

The trust discloses the effect, in its own framing: interest costs fell 31.9%, but funding costs including distributions attributable to the perpetual securities fell 14.5%, or S$3.5 million. The distance between those two percentages is the coupon.

Perpetual securities are classified as equity. They do not appear in the 35.3% aggregate leverage ratio. Total borrowings at 30 June were S$1,168.5 million; borrowings plus perpetuals were S$1,418.9 million; adding the S$141.9 million of lease liabilities excluded from the borrowings table takes the figure to S$1,560.8 million. Measured against the deposited-property base implied by the reported 35.3%, those come to 42.9% and 47.1% respectively. The 42.9% and 47.1% figures are PropertyAtlas computations; CDLHT’s reported aggregate leverage of 35.3% is the Monetary Authority of Singapore basis and is correctly stated, and perpetual securities are not required to be included in it.

They do count against interest cover. The interest coverage ratio of 2.3 times is computed, on the trust’s own footnote, against trailing twelve-month interest expense, borrowing-related fees and distributions on hybrid securities. The published sensitivity puts that ratio at 2.1 times on a 10% fall in EBITDA and 1.8 times on a 100 basis point rise in the weighted average interest rate, against a regulatory minimum of 1.5 times.

So the perpetuals flatter the leverage ratio and do not flatter the coverage ratio. That is the trade the balance sheet made for a S$7.7 million interest saving and an 8.6% increase in distribution per security.

What the cash did

Net cash generated from operating activities fell 3.1%, from S$48.473 million to S$46.978 million, in the same half in which the declared distribution rose 9.7%.

Cash actually paid out during the period was S$35.898 million to stapled securityholders — the second-half 2025 distribution — and S$2.753 million to perpetual securities holders, S$38.651 million in all. Operating cash flow less capital expenditure of S$7.917 million was S$39.061 million. Cover of 1.01 times.

Just over a fifth of the declared distribution is capital rather than income. Of the 2.15 cents, 0.44 cents is a capital component — 0.41 cents from the REIT and 0.03 cents from the business trust — described in the accounts as operating cash flows from overseas properties. That component has not moved: it was 0.44 cents in the prior half-year too. The whole of the increase came from the taxable income component, which rose from 1.54 to 1.71 cents.

Books close at 5.00pm on Friday 7 August 2026. Payment is on Monday 31 August.

Liquidity, with the qualifier attached

CDLHT reports roughly S$643 million of cash and available facilities. The composition is worth separating out.

Cash and cash equivalents stood at S$87.585 million, of which S$2.108 million is restricted, leaving S$85.477 million unrestricted. Undrawn committed revolving credit was S$62.2 million, out of a S$350.0 million three-year committed facility with S$287.8 million already drawn.

The remainder is uncommitted: S$93.6 million of undrawn uncommitted revolving credit, and S$400.0 million of uncommitted multi-currency bridge loan facilities — S$300.0 million at the REIT and S$100.0 million at the business trust — which the announcement records as unutilised at 30 June. An uncommitted facility is one the lender is not obliged to fund.

On that split, around S$148 million of the headline liquidity is cash or committed undrawn credit, and roughly S$494 million — 77% — is not. Against it, S$236.949 million of borrowings fall due within twelve months. Committed liquidity covers those maturities 0.62 times.

The trust posts net current liabilities of S$177.9 million at 30 June and notes, correctly, that excluding borrowings due within a year it would show a net current asset position of S$59.2 million. It also retains S$1.25 billion of unissued capacity under the debt issuance programme and S$1.0 billion under a separate medium term note programme, neither drawn, and 95.8% of property value unencumbered.

This is a refinancing observation, not a solvency one. It is also the reason the move in fixed-rate borrowings from 66.9% at 31 March to 73.7% at 30 June matters more than it looks, and why the S$163.6 million of additional interest rate swaps entered into during the period is the more informative disclosure in the finance section.

The Maldives guarantee now has a number on it

Portfolio net property income from the Maldives fell 77.8%, from S$3.368 million to S$748,000 — the worst line in the portfolio by a distance, and a S$2.62 million swing against a group net property income that grew S$1.06 million.

Two assets sit behind it. Angsana Velavaru is master-leased to a subsidiary of Banyan Tree Holdings on a ten-year term to 31 January 2033, at a minimum rent of US$6.0 million a year. The Halcyon Private Isles Maldives, Autograph Collection — rebranded from Raffles Maldives Meradhoo on 1 November 2025 — is operated inside the business trust and produced a net property loss of S$2.395 million, against a loss of S$613,000 a year earlier.

The minimum rent is not open-ended. Top-ups under the master lease are capped at a cumulative US$6.0 million across the entire ten-year term, and once that reserve is exhausted no further top-up is payable. The financial statements disclose that the lessee had paid US$1.8 million of it by 30 June 2026.

Thirty per cent of the guarantee has been consumed. About 34% of the lease term has elapsed. On the arithmetic that is proportionate — but the consumption is concentrated in the recent period, and the outlook in the trust’s own commentary is for continued pressure: Maldives arrivals down 5.0% for the half, regional air connectivity disrupted by Middle Eastern carriers suspending and reducing services, increased resort supply, and no fixed-price energy contracts available in the market. US$4.2 million of reserve is left to cover six and a half years.

One relief is available and it has a date on it. The business trust’s Maldives subsidiary contracted in 2024 to purchase a minimum number of seaplane charter flights at S$1.6 million, or US$1.3 million, a year to 31 October 2029 — a fixed cost carried against a resort currently loss-making at the net property income line. The agreement is cancellable by either party, without cause, after 31 October 2026.

The master lease, and the question it leaves open

Back to the renewal, because it is the structural fact in this release.

The master lease covering Orchard Hotel, Grand Copthorne Waterfront Hotel, M Hotel and Copthorne King’s Hotel ran for twenty years from 19 July 2006 and expired on 18 July 2026. The results presentation now carries a term of twenty years from 19 July 2026, expiring 18 July 2046, on the same formula: rent of 20% of revenue plus 20% of gross operating profit, subject to an annual fixed rent floor of S$26.4 million — S$13.4 million of fixed rent and S$13.0 million of service charge.

The lessee is a related corporation of the manager. Rental income received from related corporations of the REIT manager was S$34.597 million for the half, 27.3% of group revenue.

What the results documents do not disclose is whether that S$26.4 million floor moved. The formula is presented as unchanged. The floor is presented as a current figure, not as a comparison with the lease that has just expired. For an arrangement newly fixed for twenty years over four of the six hotels in a market that is 62.0% of portfolio value, the level of that floor is the single most consequential number in the release — and it arrives without a prior-period comparative, in an appendix, unremarked.

That is a question for the manager, and a line to watch for in the full-year report.

Grand Millennium Auckland was the standout, with net property income up 60.4% on completed public-area and guestroom upgrades and stronger conference demand; the trust puts the increase at 27.1% once a straight-line rent effect in the prior period is removed. Perth added 50.2% on margin improvement and the repositioning of ibis Perth’s food and beverage outlets, despite RevPAR up only 0.8%. The United Kingdom rose 17.8%, but entirely on the living assets — The Castings and Benson Yard together added S$1.9 million, more than doubling, while the four UK hotels were flat at S$7.0 million as business rate increases, a large part of them prior-period adjustments, absorbed a 2.3% RevPAR gain.

Against that, Japan fell 11.5% — only 2.8% in local currency — on Chinese arrivals down 56.4% amid bilateral tensions, with a tripled departure tax and a fivefold increase in short-term visa fees taking effect on 1 July. Germany fell 13.1% on the absence of the citywide events that supported the prior second quarter. Italy rose 11.5% against a low base created by a three-week closure for pipe works in January 2025.

Note that Germany and Italy are both reported on straight-lined rent below the contractual level — €3.1 million against a €3.6 million fixed rent in Munich, €1.1 million against €1.3 million in Florence — so the reported declines understate the cash position at both.

What to watch

Three asset enhancement programmes overlap. M Hotel began a full renovation of all 415 rooms in May 2026, running in phases to completion in the third quarter of 2027; 4,439 room nights came out of inventory in the half. Copthorne King’s Hotel starts on 167 main-wing rooms in the fourth quarter, on a land lease with 41 years remaining. Hilton Cambridge City Centre begins a full renovation of all 198 guestrooms plus food and beverage and public areas, also in the fourth quarter. Three of the trust’s larger assets will be partially out of service simultaneously through 2027.

Moxy Singapore Clarke Quay remains the open capital commitment: 475 keys, acquired by forward purchase at the lower of S$475.0 million or 110% of the vendor’s development cost, with temporary occupation permit expected late 2026 and completion in the first half of 2027. It would lift Singapore keys from 2,555 to 3,030. That commitment is roughly 48% of a market capitalisation of about S$1.0 billion, and roughly half the S$968 million of gearing headroom to the 50% limit — and it does not appear in the S$11.3 million of capital expenditure contracted but not provided for at 30 June.

Singapore supply adds 3,237 rooms between August 2026 and 2028, 62% of it upscale or luxury, against stock of 74,929 — a modest 1.8% compound rate, but concentrated at the top of the market where CDLHT’s Singapore portfolio sits.

Net asset value per stapled security was S$1.37 at 30 June, against S$1.40 at 31 December. Stapled securities issued to the manager after period end in satisfaction of the base fee were priced at S$0.7816, or 0.57 times that net asset value.

The half did what the trust said it did. The load-bearing parts of it are in the appendices.
