CapitaLand Investment's Profit From Operations Fell 9.4 Per Cent To S$242 Million In The First Half Of 2026, While Profit Attributable To Owners Rose 13.9 Per Cent To S$327 Million — The Entire Increase Was Earned Below The Operating Line

Profit from operations fell 9.4 per cent. Profit attributable to owners rose 13.9 per cent. The gap is closed by a S$141 million reversal of Chinese land appreciation tax, a S$27 million improvement in associates and joint ventures, and S$12 million of…

By PropertyAtlas·15 August 2026·13 min read
Photo: CapitaSky at 79 Robinson Road, Singapore, an office asset of CapitaLand Integrated Commercial Trust — CapitaLand Investment's largest listed fund, whose assets under management rose from S$27.7 billion at 31st March 2026 to S$30.0 billion at 30th June 2026 while CapitaLand Investment's sponsor stake fell from 21 per cent to 20 per cent. Photo: CapitaLand Investment

CapitaLand Investment reported total revenue of S$1,018 million for the six months to 30th June 2026, down 2.1 per cent, and profit attributable to owners of S$327 million, up 13.9 per cent. Between those two figures sits a line the release does not mention: profit from operations fell 9.4 per cent, to S$242 million from S$267 million. Every dollar of the increase in attributable profit was earned below the operating line.

The bridge is short and it closes exactly. Profit from operations was S$25 million worse. Finance costs were S$12 million lower. The share of results from associates and joint ventures was S$27 million higher. Those three carry profit before tax from S$353 million to S$367 million. Then the tax line supplies S$30 million, non-controlling interests take S$4 million more, and attributable profit lands at S$327 million.

Gross profit rose 12.1 per cent on lower revenue, because cost of sales fell S$79 million against a S$22 million revenue decline. Gross margin widened to 52.06 per cent from 45.48 per cent as the mix moved from property income towards fees. Administrative expenses then took S$26 million of that and other operating expenses took S$49 million, which is how a S$57 million improvement in gross profit becomes a S$25 million deterioration at the operating line.

One Hundred And Forty-One Million Dollars Of Chinese Land Tax

The S$30 million tax swing is not a rate effect. Note 7 of the financial statements sets out the components: current tax of S$111 million against S$36 million a year earlier, deferred tax of S$22 million against S$5 million, withholding tax of S$27 million against S$8 million — and a land appreciation tax line of negative S$141 million, a reversal of provision recognised on completion of the disposal of the final phase of a development-for-sale property in China.

Without that reversal the tax charge is S$160 million rather than S$19 million. Attributable profit rose S$40 million. The single Chinese tax provision released in the half is more than three times that increase. The group discloses the item plainly and in full; neither the news release nor the results presentation refers to it.

The reversal is not treated as a portfolio item. Both the financial statements and the presentation cite the completion of the development-for-sale disposal among the drivers of the 12.7 per cent rise in operating PATMI to S$293 million, the measure defined to exclude portfolio gains, revaluations and impairments.

The Second Quarter, On Its Own

CapitaLand Investment publishes a first-quarter business update and a half-year result. It does not publish a second quarter. Subtracting the one from the other gives it, and every segment re-sums to the disclosed totals.

Recurring Growth Halved While The Headline Tripled

Both presentations split each segment’s fee revenue into recurring and event-driven bars. Read across the two decks, the split does something the half-year figures conceal.

Recurring fee revenue grew 9.6 per cent in the first quarter and 4.9 per cent in the second. The headline grew 10.3 per cent and then 29.6 per cent. The two lines move in opposite directions. Event-driven fees went from S$12 million to S$78 million between the quarters, and from 3.9 per cent of quarterly fee revenue to 20.7 per cent.

Across the half the same arithmetic gives recurring fee revenue of S$597 million against S$557 million, up 7.2 per cent, and event-driven fee revenue of S$90 million against S$15 million. Event-driven fees supplied 65.2 per cent of the S$115 million increase. The presentation’s opening slide labels the S$687 million total “Growing Recurring Fee Revenue”; the group’s own segmental slide puts S$90 million of it in the event-driven bars. The S$78 million second-quarter figure includes an S$8 million performance fee from an India fund, which is recognised in other operating income rather than in revenue.

The Deals Were Announced Before The End Of April

Event-driven fees follow transactions. The transactions are disclosed twice, on comparable definitions, four months apart. Listed-fund acquisitions announced between 1st January and 28th April 2026 totalled S$6.9 billion. Listed-fund acquisitions announced between 1st January and 12th August 2026 totalled S$7.1 billion. Roughly S$0.2 billion was added across three and a half months. Divestments moved from S$2.9 billion to S$3.5 billion over the same span. The first-quarter figures exclude CapitaLand Commercial C-REIT and Japan Hotel REIT; the half-year figures exclude Japan Hotel REIT only, so the comparison is close rather than exact.

The deal flow that produced the second quarter’s fees was therefore substantially complete by April, while the fees themselves were recognised in the June quarter. The group states the position directly in its outlook: transaction-related activity may moderate from the strong levels recorded in the first half.

There is one further wrinkle. The variance note records that the half-year operating performance was broadly in line with the guidance given on 29th April 2026 — guidance which had warned that uncertain conditions were expected to moderate the pace of capital raising and deployment. The quarter that followed grew fee revenue almost three times faster than the quarter that preceded it.

What The Fee Revenue Figure Counts

Fee related revenue of S$687 million is not a line in the financial statements. It reconciles to one exactly. The segment note puts fee income-related business revenue at S$665 million, made up of Listed Funds Management S$217 million, Private Funds Management S$77 million, Commercial Management S$198 million and Lodging Management S$173 million. Adding CapitaLand Investment’s 40 per cent share of SC Capital Partners fee revenue of S$14 million and the S$8 million India performance fee gives S$687 million. The prior half reconciles the same way: S$564 million plus S$8 million of SC Capital Partners gives S$572 million.

The basis moved between the two halves, and the presentation says so. The comparative included only the SC Capital Partners share; the current period adds the performance fee. On a like-for-like basis excluding the performance fee, fee related revenue rose 18.7 per cent rather than 20.1 per cent.

The distinction matters most in private funds. The presentation reports Private Funds Management fee revenue up 59 per cent, from S$58 million to S$92 million. On the group’s own revenue in the segment note, the movement is S$54 million to S$77 million, or 42.6 per cent. The difference is the SC Capital Partners share and the performance fee. Total fee income-related business revenue rose 17.9 per cent against the headline 20.1 per cent.

China’s revenue fell 5.0 per cent while its EBITDA fell 66.2 per cent. The group attributes the gap to realisation of foreign currency translation reserve and negative hedging reserve losses on China investments, together with the loss of contribution from a divested business park. Singapore’s S$91 million improvement came largely from Listed Funds Management fees and from the share of realised fair value gain on the divestment of Asia Square Tower 2. Other developed markets lost 28.2 per cent of revenue, chiefly the deconsolidation of Synergy following the SilverDoor merger, but only S$6 million of EBITDA.

The Fee Business Earns More On One-Seventh Of The Assets

The segment note discloses assets as well as earnings. Fee income-related business assets were S$3,144 million at 30th June 2026 and produced S$271 million of EBITDA. Real estate investment business assets were S$20,026 million and produced S$303 million. That is a half-year EBITDA return of 8.62 per cent on fee-business assets against 1.51 per cent on investment-business assets. The fee business holds 13.2 per cent of segment assets and generated 46.6 per cent of EBITDA.

Real estate investment business revenue fell 24.5 per cent across the half, and the decline is accelerating. Applying the same first-quarter subtraction, the segment’s revenue fell 14.5 per cent in the first quarter and 33.2 per cent in the second. Within it, non-fund investments — subsidiaries, associates and joint ventures outside the listed and private funds — fell 41.1 per cent in the second quarter. This is the arithmetic behind the strategy the group announced alongside the result.

What It Costs To Raise REIT Equity

Other operating expenses of S$89 million against S$40 million is the largest single deterioration on the face of the income statement. Note 6(d) puts S$84 million of it in net loss from change of ownership interests in subsidiaries, associates and joint ventures, against S$17 million. The performance review attributes the increase to realisation of translation reserve losses on the China disposal and to higher dilution losses from REIT private placements.

Those dilution losses have a visible counterpart. The listed funds platform table is published in both presentations, four months apart.

The S$2.3 billion of listed-fund equity raised in the period, presented as enhanced capital availability, is the same activity that produced the dilution charge in other operating expenses. The presentation reports it once as an achievement and the accounts report it once as a cost.

The platform tables also decompose the funds under management figure the group does not break down. Private funds fell from S$50 billion to S$49 billion, while the number of funds rose from 62 to 64 and total equity invested rose from S$30 billion to S$31 billion. All of the increase in the S$128 billion headline is listed.

Cash, Dividends And A Negative Current Position

Net cash generated from operating activities was S$118 million, against S$74 million. Dividends paid to shareholders in the half were S$599 million, the final dividend of 12.0 cents for 2025 approved in April and paid in May. The distribution was 5.08 times net operating cash flow. No interim dividend was declared, consistent with the group’s stated practice of paying a first and final dividend only.

The presentation reports operating cash flow of S$448 million, a figure that does not appear in the cash flow statement. Its footnote explains the difference and the arithmetic confirms it: net cash from operating activities of S$118 million plus S$330 million of dividends and distributions received from associates, joint ventures and other investments, which the cash flow statement classifies as investing. The prior half reconciles identically at S$74 million plus S$237 million against a stated S$311 million.

The group moved from net current assets of S$964 million to net current liabilities of S$58 million, a swing of S$1,022 million in six months. The basis of preparation note addresses it directly: the position arises from the reclassification of borrowings from long-term to short-term in line with their repayment terms, and the group holds approximately S$6.1 billion of cash and undrawn facilities in its treasury vehicles. Debt securities of S$588 million became current, against none at December. Of on-balance-sheet debt due in 2026, the presentation states that S$0.2 billion is to be repaid and none requires refinancing.

Two Figures That Do Not Reconcile

The performance review states operating EBITDA of S$559 million, against S$566 million. The presentation splits total EBITDA of S$581 million into fee business S$271 million, real estate investment business operating S$271 million, real estate investment business non-operating S$32 million and corporate and others S$7 million. The operating components sum to S$549 million, not S$559 million; for the prior half they sum to S$580 million against a stated S$566 million. Neither document sets out the bridge between the two measures.

Portfolio gains are also given twice. The overview table reports S$34 million against S$27 million, which reconciles to attributable profit alongside operating PATMI of S$293 million and S$260 million. The EBITDA discussion on the same page reports portfolio gains of S$23 million against S$16 million. The two are consistent with measurement at different levels of the income statement, but the document does not say which is which.

The Non-Core Question

The strategic announcement made alongside the result divides the group into a core business of listed and private funds, and a non-core business of legacy funds, balance sheet investments and non-strategic holdings in the group’s own REITs and private funds. The non-core portfolio is said to hold approximately S$7 billion to S$9 billion of embedded value for recycling and reinvestment, with excess capital returned to shareholders.

The presentation values effective stakes at S$8.2 billion in listed funds, S$5.1 billion in private funds and S$3.9 billion in non-fund investments, a total of S$17.2 billion. The non-core range is therefore between 40.7 and 52.3 per cent of the disclosed value of effective stakes, and between 55.8 and 71.8 per cent of the S$12,538 million of equity attributable to owners. The group has not stated how the two definitions overlap, and the ranges should be read as scale rather than as a reconciliation.

Some of that recycling is already visible. Gross divestments of approximately S$5.0 billion were announced year to date. The group deconsolidated CLACP II LP in April after selling a 23.44 per cent interest, and now accounts for it as an associate. The China Commercial Private REIT, seeded with CapitaMall LuOne in Shanghai, listed on 11th August at RMB3.15 billion, or approximately S$592 million; a second C-REIT has regulatory approval and is targeted for the second half.

What The Half Establishes

The asset-light case is well made by the segment note: a fee business earning nearly six times the return on assets of the investment business, and taking 46.6 per cent of EBITDA from 13.2 per cent of segment assets. Funds under management reached S$128 billion, all-in fee revenue against funds under management improved to 88 basis points from 85, and the implied borrowing cost fell to 3.5 per cent.

What the half does not establish is that the earnings growth repeats. Recurring fee revenue grew 4.9 per cent in the second quarter. The transactions that generated the event-driven fees were announced by April. The operating line went backwards, and the tax line that carried attributable profit forward was a provision release on a completed Chinese disposal. On the group’s own guidance, transaction-related activity may moderate from here.

Earnings per share were 6.6 cents against 5.8 cents. Net asset value per share was S$2.51 against S$2.52 at December. The figures have neither been audited nor reviewed.
