CapitaLand China Trust's DPU Fell Only 1.6% While Total Return Attributable to Unitholders Fell 11.1%. Distribution Adjustments Now Carry 37.6% of the Payout, and the Distribution Is 1.59 Times Earnings

CapitaLand China Trust's DPU fell 1.6% to 2.45 cents while total return attributable to unitholders fell 11.1%. Distribution adjustments now carry 37.6% of the payout.

By PropertyAtlas·5 August 2026·11 min read
Photo: CapitaMall Xizhimen in Xicheng District, Beijing — CapitaLand China Trust's largest asset at 38.4% of unitholders' funds and 99.4% committed occupancy at 30 June 2026. Beijing carries 51.4% of the trust's retail valuation, in a city whose own retail sales fell 2.2% over the half. Credit: CapitaLand China Trust.

CapitaLand China Trust (CLCT) reported a distribution per unit of 2.45 Singapore cents for the half year ended 30 June 2026, down 1.6%. Total return attributable to unitholders over the same half fell 11.1%. The distribution held because distribution adjustments now carry 37.6% of it. Aggregate leverage improved by three-tenths of a point, and the exchange rate accounts for almost all of that.

Financial Headlines

Gross revenue S$152.287 million, down 4.4%. Net property income S$103.863 million, down 2.5%. In renminbi, where the assets sit, revenue fell 5.2% and net property income 3.3% — the smaller Singapore-dollar declines are the currency, not the buildings. Amount available for distribution S$43.165 million, down 4.4%. Distributable amount to unitholders S$43.165 million, down 0.6%, the difference being that the prior half retained S$1.754 million attributable to CapitaLand Consumption C-REIT. DPU 2.45 cents, a tax-exempt income distribution. Net asset value per unit S$1.04. Aggregate leverage 40.4%. Interest coverage 2.9 times. Average cost of debt 3.06%. Average term to maturity 3.0 years. Portfolio 8 retail malls, 5 business parks and 4 logistics parks across 11 cities, carried at S$4.322 billion. Ex-date 13 August, record date 14 August, payment 9 September 2026.

The distribution held. The earnings behind it did not.

The headline reads as a portfolio holding its ground: distributable amount down 0.6%, DPU down 1.6%. One line down, the picture is different. Total return for the period attributable to unitholders fell from S$30.304 million to S$26.932 million, a decline of 11.1%. What closes the gap between an 11.1% fall in return and a 0.6% fall in the payout is distribution adjustments, which rose 9.2% to S$16.233 million.

Those adjustments are now 37.6% of distributable income, up from 32.9% a year ago. On a per-unit basis the same fact reads more starkly. DPU of 2.45 cents sits against basic earnings per unit of 1.54 cents: the distribution is 1.59 times earnings, widened from 1.42 times in the prior half. Both figures are disclosed; neither appears in the results presentation.

Deferred tax is the largest adjustment, and it is accruing on the balance sheet

Deferred taxation of S$11.557 million is the single biggest add-back, on its own 26.8% of the distributable amount. Together with the S$6.422 million of manager’s fees settled in units rather than cash, the two lines are S$17.979 million — 41.7% of everything CLCT distributed.

Neither is a criticism of the accounting. Both are standard, both are disclosed, and both are how nearly every China-exposed S-REIT presents. But deferred taxation is not a timing artefact that disappears. Over the same six months the deferred tax liability on the balance sheet rose S$20.908 million to S$377.952 million, which is 20.6% of unitholders’ funds. Money added back to the distribution today is money the structure expects to owe on realisation. The effective tax rate for the half was 44.3%, up from 42.5%.

Manager’s fees in units carry their own cost. Units in issue rose from 1,740,902,808 to 1,757,150,191 in six months, an increase of 0.93%, entirely from management fee settlement. A further 9,982,387 units are to be issued, taking issued and issuable to 1,767,132,578 — an overhang of 0.57% against which the DPU is not yet computed.

The same-store comparison keeps the divestment’s benefit and removes its cost

The release leads on a same-store number: excluding CapitaMall Yuhuating’s contribution from the 1H 2025 base, DPU rose 2.9% year-on-year from 2.38 cents. The arithmetic is correct. Yuhuating contributed 0.11 cents in 1H 2025, and 2.45 against 2.38 is a gain of 0.07 cents.

The comparison is one-sided in a specific way. It strips the income Yuhuating produced out of the base, but it keeps in the current period the benefit that selling Yuhuating financed. Finance costs fell S$5.159 million, which the Manager attributes to a lower average cost of debt and the repayment of Trust loans using the divestment proceeds. On 1,750,204,000 weighted average units, S$5.159 million is 0.29 cents — more than four times the same-store gain. That per-unit conversion is a PropertyAtlas derivation, not a disclosed figure, and the Manager attributes only part of the saving to the proceeds.

None of which makes the underlying trend negative. The sequential path is the honest version and it is genuinely better: 2.38 cents in 1H 2025 on a like-for-like basis, 2.00 cents of organic distribution in 2H 2025 before a 0.33-cent top-up, and 2.45 cents now with no top-up at all. Against the organic second half, this is a 22.5% recovery.

What the retained 5% has done

CLCT kept a 5% stake in CapitaLand Consumption C-REIT when Yuhuating went across on 31 October 2025. In this half that stake produced investment income of S$283,000, relating to distributions from 16 September to 31 December 2025. Over the same period its carrying value fell from S$23.875 million to S$21.956 million, a decline of 8.0%, which the Manager attributes to the C-REIT’s share price. Net of the income, the retained interest is S$1.636 million lighter than at 31 December. The fair value movement went to reserves rather than through the distribution.

One naming point worth carrying: the vehicle formerly reported as CapitaLand Commercial C-REIT is now CapitaLand Consumption C-REIT. The glossary records the change; the ticker of the exposure has not moved, only its name.

Gearing fell three-tenths of a point, and currency explains almost all of it

Aggregate leverage was 40.4% at 30 June 2026 against 40.7% at 31 December 2025 and 42.1% a year ago. The half-on-half improvement is 0.3 percentage points.

The presentation discloses its own sensitivity: a 1% move in SGD/RMB shifts aggregate leverage by 0.1 percentage points. Over the same six months the closing rate moved from 5.466 to 5.321, a renminbi appreciation of 2.7%. Applying the Manager’s own sensitivity, currency accounts for roughly 0.27 of the 0.3-point improvement. On the year-on-year comparison the disclosed 4.2% appreciation accounts for about 0.42 of the 1.7-point fall, with debt repayment doing the rest. The half-on-half derivation is PropertyAtlas’s, applying a disclosed sensitivity to a disclosed rate move.

Nothing was revalued

Investment properties rose from S$4,204.374 million to S$4,322.086 million, an increase of S$117.712 million. The note breaks it down completely: S$114.906 million of translation differences, S$2.806 million of capitalised expenditure, and a change in fair value of exactly nil. In renminbi the portfolio moved from RMB 22,981.0 million to RMB 22,995.9 million — the capital expenditure and nothing else.

The Manager conducted an internal assessment in consultation with its panel of external valuers and concluded that fair value had not materially changed from the 31 December 2025 independent valuation. That is a defensible interim position and a common one. It is also worth stating plainly: the S$4.3 billion carrying value in this announcement is a December mark carried forward and translated, and the portfolio statement footnote says so. The last external round produced a fair value loss of S$50.507 million for FY 2025. The malls at the weaker end — CapitaMall Xinnan at 86.9% committed occupancy in a Chengdu market the Manager itself flags for lower rates — have not been externally marked since December.

The funding mix moved sharply in a single quarter

Renminbi-denominated debt went from 41% a year ago to 59% at 31 March and 73% at 30 June — 14 points in one quarter. Including forward contracts, 92% of total debt is now renminbi. As a natural hedge against renminbi assets this is straightforwardly good, and it is the clearest deliberate capital action in the half.

It came with a trade. Borrowings on fixed interest rates fell from 87% to 71% over the year. The release presents the 71% as mitigation of interest rate movements, which it is, but the direction of travel is 16 points the other way. The disclosed sensitivity now reads: a 50 basis point move in renminbi rates shifts distribution by S$2.1 million, 0.12 cents, or 2.5% of DPU; the same move in Singapore dollar rates is worth 0.03 cents. The floating exposure has become almost entirely a renminbi exposure.

Interest coverage has been 2.9 times at three consecutive reported dates. Under the Manager’s own sensitivities, a 100 basis point rise in weighted average rates takes it to 2.3 times, and a 10% fall in EBITDA takes it to 2.6 times.

The distribution is disclosed as part-funded by borrowings

Section 5 of the announcement states that the tax-exempt income distribution for the period is expected to be funded from borrowings at the Trust level as well as internal cash flows from operations. The cash flow statement is consistent with that. Net cash from operating activities was S$79.064 million. Interest paid was S$24.786 million, capital expenditure S$5.864 million. Net of those and of interest received, free cash was approximately S$48.711 million against S$52.391 million of distributions actually paid to unitholders, non-controlling interests and perpetual securities holders — cover of 0.93 times.

The balance sheet shows where the funding came from. Current interest-bearing borrowings rose from S$113.906 million to S$294.352 million, and at Trust level from nil to S$177.164 million, which the notes attribute to loans drawn for working capital. Current liabilities now exceed current assets by S$200.893 million, against S$10.363 million at 31 December. The Manager states it has S$516 million of undrawn revolving facilities and expresses the opinion that it can refinance and meet obligations as they fall due. The nearest hard maturity is the FTZ bond of RMB 600 million at 3.8%, roughly S$112.8 million, in October 2026.

Retail is genuinely working, and the release describes it accurately

The operating story in the retail portfolio, which is 70.6% of gross rental income, is the strongest part of this result and is fairly reported. Committed occupancy rose to 97.3% from 96.9%. Shopper traffic rose 3.2% and tenant sales 2.6% — 7.5% excluding automobile sales, though tenant sales per square metre on that basis rose only 1.9%, below the traffic growth. Occupancy cost held at 17.5%, down from 27.5% in 2022.

The context makes it better. Beijing carries 51.4% of CLCT’s retail valuation, and Beijing’s own retail sales fell 2.2% from January to June. Citywide mall occupancy there is 92.9% and first-floor rents are down 3.0% year-on-year. CLCT’s three Beijing malls closed the half at 99.4%, 98.7% and 96.1%. The portfolio is outperforming its largest market rather than riding it.

One qualification on the drivers cited. The release names CapitaMall Xinnan, CapitaMall Grand Canyon and CapitaMall Aidemengdun for lower occupancy and rental rates. Two of the three ended the half with higher committed occupancy than a year earlier — Grand Canyon at 98.7% against 97.0%, Aidemengdun at 97.9% against 95.6%. Only Xinnan fell. At those two malls the pressure is on rate, not on occupancy.

Two of the three rental reversions are not in the news release

The news release reports one reversion figure. It is the logistics park portfolio, where proactive leasing of 18,200 square metres improved reversion from −24.5% in FY 2025 to −1.2% in 1H 2026. That is a real and substantial improvement, and logistics is 3.4% of gross rental income.

The results presentation reports two more. Retail reversion was −2.7%, or −2.3% excluding two planned anchor adjustments and an electric vehicle tenancy. Business park reversion was −12.0%. Neither number appears in the news release, which covers business parks with the volume of leasing done rather than the rate achieved. Retail and business parks together are 96.6% of gross rental income.

Business parks are 26% of income and repriced 12% down

CLCT secured approximately 102,000 square metres of renewals and new leases in its business parks, which the Manager states is about 14.3% of the portfolio’s net lettable area. Summing the five assets’ disclosed net lettable areas gives 712,320 square metres, and 102,000 against that is 14.32% — the disclosure reconciles exactly. So roughly one seventh of the business park book repriced this half, at −12.0%.

Committed occupancy at 85.1% is the lowest of the five reported dates. Singapore-Hangzhou Science and Technology Park Phase II fell from 79.7% to 70.6% over the year and Phase I from 73.8% to 71.0%. Ascendas Innovation Towers recovered strongly from 74.6% a year ago but has now declined for two consecutive quarters from 85.2% at December.

The submarkets explain the pressure and the Manager sets them out fairly. Hangzhou business park stock grew 8.2% year-on-year to 25.0 million square metres, submarket occupancy is 52.0%, rents are down 12.4%, and a further 3.7 million square metres is expected by year end. Suzhou stock grew 7.9%, submarket occupancy is 67.8%, rents down 6.8%. Against that, CLCT’s assets are ahead of their submarkets on every comparison shown — Xinsu at 95.4% against 73.1%, the Xi’an pair at 83.0% against 81.6%, the Hangzhou pair at 70.8% against 52.0%.

Two things sharpen the risk. Business park weighted average lease expiry is 1.8 years on both gross rental income and net lettable area, the shortest of the three segments, and 55.9% of business park income expires by the end of 2027. And the city hosting Ascendas Innovation Towers and Ascendas Innovation Hub, 22.5% of business park valuation, recorded GDP growth of 1.1% in the first quarter — the weakest figure in the Manager’s own city table by a wide margin. Xi’an, Harbin and Hohhot are the three cities reported on a first-quarter rather than first-half basis, and they are the three slowest shown.

The expiry profile puts the expensive leases first

The retail lease expiry table discloses each year twice, once as a share of gross rental income and once as a share of net lettable area. Dividing one by the other gives the rent density of each expiry cohort relative to the portfolio average, and the result is a clean descending line. The leases expiring soonest are the ones paying the most.

Leases representing 18.6% of retail income expire in 2026 across just 13.0% of area — a rent density of 1.43 times the portfolio average. The 2027 cohort, 975 leases and 36.7% of retail income, sits at 1.22 times. At the far end, 23.2% of net lettable area expiring beyond 2030 contributes only 8.2% of income, a density of 0.35 — the long anchor leases. Taken together, 55.3% of retail gross rental income reprices within eighteen months, at rent levels well above the portfolio average, into a current reversion of −2.7%. The 7.4% yield includes a top-up

The stated distribution yield of 7.4% is built on trailing twelve-month DPU of 4.78 cents against a unit price of S$0.650. The footnote discloses that the 4.78 includes a distribution top-up of 0.33 cents paid in the second half of 2025. Stripping it out gives 4.45 cents and a yield of 6.85% — about 50 basis points below the headline. The disclosure is complete and sits directly beneath the number; the qualification simply does not travel with the figure once it is quoted.

Where the cash went

Cash and cash equivalents fell S$20.885 million despite S$79.064 million from operations. A large part of the difference is a set of movements with the non-controlling interests in Ascendas Xinsu Portfolio and the Hangzhou and Xi’an vehicles. CLCT advanced S$21.182 million to non-controlling interests, recorded as a receivable and described as facilitating more efficient deployment of cash within the portfolio. It also paid S$8.890 million of distributions to them, against S$2.807 million a year earlier, and repaid S$6.414 million of shareholder loan.

The three together are S$36.486 million of cash moved to non-controlling interests in six months, against S$40.563 million distributed to unitholders, and against a non-controlling share of total return of S$6.321 million. The S$21.182 million is an advance rather than a cost and is expected back. The comparison is not a like-for-like one. But it is the single largest reason the cash balance fell in a half where operations produced nearly S$80 million.

What to watch

The FTZ bond of RMB 600 million at 3.8% maturing in October, and whether it is refinanced from the S$516 million of undrawn revolving facilities or termed out.

The 31 December 2026 independent valuation, which will be the first external mark since December 2025 and the first to see CapitaMall Xinnan at 86.9% and Singapore-Hangzhou Phase II at 70.6%.

Business park reversion. At −12.0% with 55.9% of segment income expiring by end-2027 and Hangzhou submarket occupancy at 52.0%, this is the line that decides whether the retail recovery carries the portfolio or is offset by it.

Whether the fixed-rate proportion is rebuilt from 71%, given that a 100 basis point move now costs about 0.30 cents of DPU on the disclosed sensitivities, roughly 6% of the current payout.

And whether the acquisition intent becomes a transaction. The Manager repeated that it will seek retail acquisitions in Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities and recycle mature assets. With gearing at 40.4% and improving mainly on currency, the funding route for any purchase is the question the strategy slide does not answer.
