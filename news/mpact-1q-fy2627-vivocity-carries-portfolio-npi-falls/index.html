MPACT 1Q FY26/27: VivoCity Lifts Net Property Income 8.9% and Reverses Rents 13.5% While the Portfolio Falls 6.8% — Committed Occupancy Drops Five Points to 84.4% on a Single Emptied Building in Chiba, and Lower Finance Costs Do More for the Distribution Than the Portfolio Does

Gross revenue fell 5.6% to S$206.5 million and net property income 6.8% to S$154.8 million, yet the distribution held within 2.5% at 1.96 Singapore cents. On the Manager’s own comparable basis, portfolio net property income fell S$7.6 million — and…

By PropertyAtlas·30 July 2026·7 min read
Photo: The VC20 installation at VivoCity, Singapore, which delivered 8.9% net property income growth and a 13.5% rental reversion in the first quarter of FY26/27 while marking its twentieth year. Credit: Mapletree Pan Asia Commercial Trust.

Mapletree Pan Asia Commercial Trust closed the first quarter of FY26/27 with gross revenue down 5.6% year-on-year to S$206.5 million and net property income down 6.8% to S$154.8 million, yet the distribution held within 2.5% at 1.96 Singapore cents. On the Manager's own comparable basis, portfolio net property income fell S$7.6 million. VivoCity added S$4.1 million against it. Every other line in the portfolio declined.

One mall did most of the work

MPACT reported its first quarter to 30 June 2026 on 30 July. The amount available for distribution eased 2.4% to S$104.2 million and distribution per unit 2.5% to 1.96 cents. On a constant-currency basis the Manager puts the revenue and net property income declines at 4.8% and 6.3%.

The headline is a portfolio in contraction. The composition underneath it is narrower than that, and more interesting.

The Manager publishes a comparable-basis bridge that strips TS Ikebukuro, ABAS Shin-Yokohama and the Festival Walk office tower — all divested across FY25/26 — out of both periods. On that basis portfolio net property income fell from S$164.6 million to S$157.0 million. VivoCity went from S$45.4 million to S$49.5 million.

The six declining lines total S$11.8 million between them. VivoCity absorbed just over a third of that. Without the mall, the comparable-basis decline would have been S$11.8 million rather than S$7.6 million, and the distribution arithmetic would have looked considerably less orderly.

What VivoCity actually delivered

Net property income at VivoCity rose 8.9% year-on-year. Rental reversion came in at 13.5% across 67 leases and 218,900 square feet, tenant retention at 76.5%, and committed occupancy at 99.7% — unchanged from March and from a year ago. Tenant sales reached S$266.8 million, up 4.9%, and shopper traffic 10.9 million, up 5.0%.

Those last two numbers are worth holding side by side. Sales rose 4.9% on traffic that rose 5.0%, which puts spend per visit at roughly S$24.48 against S$24.44 a year earlier. The mall's sales growth this quarter is footfall-led, not basket-led. That is not a criticism — footfall is the harder variable to move, and the completed Basement 2 asset enhancement initiative plus the year-long VC20 twentieth-anniversary campaign are visibly doing it — but it frames what the mall is currently converting and what it is not.

The reversion figure deserves the same treatment. Colliers puts suburban retail rents at S$21.02 per square foot per month in the second quarter, up 0.2% quarter-on-quarter, with suburban occupancy at 95.7%. A 13.5% reversion against a market moving 0.2% a quarter is not a read on current market rent. It is the mark-to-market of leases struck roughly three years ago, arriving now because retail weighted average lease expiry sits at 1.8 years. The uplift is real and it is banked. It is also, by construction, finite.

The occupancy fall is one address

Committed occupancy across the portfolio fell from 89.4% at 31 March to 84.4% at 30 June, five points in a single quarter. Singapore did not cause it.

The presentation appendix carries the explanation in a footnote rather than in the commentary: the Fujitsu Makuhari Building's disclosed lettable area is reduced to 329,023 square feet from 657,549, following Fujitsu Limited ceasing to be a tenant after 31 March 2026. The first quarter of FY26/27 is the first full quarter in which that building carries its operating cost base without its anchor income.

The segment arithmetic reflects it. On the comparable basis, Japan revenue fell from S$15.3 million to S$10.4 million and net property income from S$8.8 million to S$4.5 million — a margin of 43.3% against 57.5% a year ago. Revenue fell 32%; net property income fell 49%. The revenue decline is divestments and a stronger Singapore dollar against the yen. The margin decline is a fixed cost base meeting an empty floor plate. No committed replacement tenant is disclosed.

The Manager frames Japan as stable outside the Makuhari submarket of Chiba, and the disclosed segment data supports that framing: tenant retention 100.0%, rental reversion 0.0%, 67,300 square feet renewed. The problem is not diffuse. It is one address.

Financing did more for the distribution than the portfolio did

Net property income fell S$11.2 million year-on-year. Finance expenses fell S$9.2 million, from S$50.1 million to S$40.9 million — covering 82% of it. The amount available for distribution fell only S$2.6 million as a result.

The saving is not incidental. The Manager redeemed S$250 million of 3.50% perpetual securities in June 2026 and issued S$200 million of 2.53% fixed-rate notes due 2033 in the same month. Weighted average all-in cost of debt fell to 2.94% per annum from 3.16% at March and 3.32% a year ago. Interest coverage improved to 3.3 times from 2.9. Gross debt stands at S$5,845.3 million, aggregate leverage at 37.7%, fixed-rate debt at 77.4%, and average term to maturity at 3.3 years. Available liquidity is around S$0.6 billion and no financial year carries more than 21% of debt maturing.

The perpetual redemption also cleans the capital stack: perpetual securities holders fall from S$249.1 million at 31 March to nil, with net asset value per unit unchanged at S$1.73. Moody's rating remains Baa2 with a negative outlook, as it has at the last two reporting dates.

This is the honest read on the quarter. The distribution held because of financing decisions taken over the preceding twelve months, not because of what the portfolio produced in these three months. The Manager says as much. It is worth being explicit that the same lever cannot be pulled twice at the same size — the Manager's own sensitivity is that every 50 basis point move in benchmark rates is worth about 0.11 cents of annual distribution per unit.

Overseas reversions are the occupancy trade being made

Portfolio rental reversion was positive 4.3% across 148 leases and 882,400 square feet, with retention at 80.2%. The dispersion behind that average is wide, and the negative end of it is deliberate.

China's −29.2% sits on 175,200 square feet, the second-largest re-let block in the portfolio after MBC's 249,200. The Manager describes the posture plainly as prioritising occupancy and stability amid market softness. The market data supports the necessity: Colliers puts Lufthansa Grade A rents in Beijing at RMB192.5 per square metre per month, down 3.7% quarter-on-quarter with submarket occupancy at 72.9%, while Shanghai business park rents at Zhangjiang fell 1.7% quarter-on-quarter against roughly 3.8 million square metres of new supply projected across 2026 to 2028.

Festival Walk's −9.1% sits on a much smaller 21,100 square feet, with retention at 54.7%, the lowest in the portfolio. Tenant sales there rose 4.0% to HK$863.6 million on traffic up 3.0% to 7.8 million, which puts spend per visit up about 1.4% — customers trading up modestly even as headline rents reset down.

A small piece of capital work worth noticing

Festival Walk completed an 18,800 square foot reconfiguration across three floors in July 2026, converting a single-tenant space into six curated food and beverage, lifestyle and beauty and health concepts, two on each of UG, L1 and L2. The Manager puts the return on a stabilised revenue basis at close to 50%, against capital expenditure of approximately HK$5.3 million.

That is a modest sum against a S$15.2 billion portfolio and it will not move a quarterly number on its own. The ratio is what makes it worth recording: five of the six concepts are new to the mall, and the exercise converts one covenant into six. In a mall running 54.7% retention, that is a change in the risk profile of the space, not just a yield exercise.

Concentration is the number to keep

Singapore accounts for 61% of assets under management and 66% of net property income. Within that, VivoCity alone is 27% of assets under management and 29% of net property income, and its valuation rose 5.4% year-on-year to S$4,062.0 million at 31 March 2026, or S$3,752 per square foot of lettable area on a 4.40% capitalisation rate. MBC across its two components is a further 27% of assets under management. Two Singapore addresses are more than half the trust.

The Singapore portfolio as a whole rose 3.1% to S$9,291.0 million. Overseas valuations fell 9.2% to S$5,920.1 million, of which S$301.1 million is foreign exchange translation and S$301.7 million genuine valuation movement — an almost exact split. Assets under management stand at S$15.2 billion across 15 properties and 10.2 million square feet, against a market capitalisation of S$6.8 billion at a closing unit price of S$1.29 on 30 June 2026.

What to watch

  • The committed lease at Mapletree Business City that the Manager says will begin contributing rental income later this year. It is the stated reason Singapore should stay resilient, and the only disclosed forward income event of size.
  • Fujitsu Makuhari. A building at half its disclosed lettable area with no announced replacement tenant is a fixed cost with no offset, and the Japan margin does not repair until that changes.
  • Whether VivoCity's spend per visit starts to follow its footfall. Traffic growth of 5% on a flat basket is a mall winning share of visits, not yet share of wallet.
  • How much reversion runway is left, given 1.8 years of retail weighted average lease expiry against a suburban market rent growing 0.2% a quarter.
  • Whether the China posture holds at −29.2% reversions, or whether occupancy is allowed to slip in exchange for rent.
  • Whether the Moody's negative outlook on the Baa2 rating resolves in either direction now that leverage, coverage and cost of debt have all moved the right way.
