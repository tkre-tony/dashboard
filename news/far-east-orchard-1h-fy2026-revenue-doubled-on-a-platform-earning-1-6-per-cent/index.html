Far East Orchard 1H FY2026: The UK Platform Added S$101.4M of Revenue and Slightly Less Operating Profit Than It Earned as a 49% Associate

Revenue more than doubled and profit fell. Both are true, and neither is the story — the story is a platform running at a 1.6 per cent operating margin.

By PropertyAtlas·9 August 2026·11 min read
Photo: Orchard Rendezvous Hotel, 1 Tanglin Road, Singapore — a 388-key freehold hotel leased and managed by Far East Orchard, and one of the Singapore hospitality assets whose share of group revenue fell from 35.4 per cent to 15.0 per cent as the UK platform consolidated. Credit: Far East Orchard Limited.

Far East Orchard Limited reported revenue of S$248.5 million for the six months to 30 June 2026, more than double the S$91.3 million of a year earlier, and profit after income tax of S$11.6 million, down 35.6 per cent from S$18.0 million. The revenue is real and it is mostly British. The profit decline is almost entirely the absence of a one-off gain booked in the comparative period. What sits between the two is a management platform that turned S$101.4 million of revenue into S$1.7 million of operating profit.

The Group has been executing FEOR30, its five-year strategy launched in November 2025, and this is the first reporting period in which the shape of it is visible in the accounts rather than in the narrative. Homes for Students Limited, the UK student accommodation operator in which Far East Orchard took its stake from 49 per cent to 84 per cent on 30 September 2025, is now consolidated for a full half. The effect on the top line is the largest single movement in the company’s recent reporting history. The effect below the top line is more modest than the headline suggests, and the company says so.

A Singapore-listed company established in 1967 now earns 71.4 per cent of its revenue in the United Kingdom, against 34.3 per cent a year ago. Singapore has fallen from 35.4 per cent to 15.0 per cent — not because Singapore shrank, but because it grew 15.7 per cent while the UK line grew more than fivefold.

The balance sheet did not follow. Non-current assets are still 46.8 per cent Singapore and 33.0 per cent United Kingdom. Revenue has moved to Britain; the assets have not. That divergence is the asset-light shape FEOR30 describes, and it is the most durable thing in this set of accounts.

This is the central fact of the half. In the comparative period the segment carried almost no revenue and produced S$1.721 million of total operating profit, of which S$1.950 million was the equity-accounted share of Homes for Students as a 49 per cent associate. In the current period the same business is consolidated, contributes S$101.4 million of revenue, and produces S$1.666 million — 3.2 per cent less. The segment operating margin is 1.64 per cent.

The company is candid about the cause. Its media release calls the business low-margin and attributes the offset to non-cash amortisation of intangibles recognised in the HFS purchase price allocation. It does not put a number on either. The number is S$2.340 million of amortisation charged to that segment in the current half against nil in the comparative. Added back, segment operating profit is S$4.006 million, up 132.8 per cent, at a margin of 3.95 per cent. Both readings are fair. Neither makes this a high-margin business.

At group level the arithmetic is the same story: total operating profit of S$31.2 million on S$248.5 million of revenue is a margin of 12.6 per cent, against 33.8 per cent a year ago. Employee compensation rose from S$17.1 million to S$58.2 million, an increase of 240.7 per cent. Consolidating an operator changes what a property company’s income statement looks like.

The capital recycling carried a 4.7 per cent gross margin

The Group completed S$50.7 million of disposals in the half — Westminster Fire Station in London and one Novena Medical Center unit — and the media release presents this as strategic capital recycling in line with the FEOR30 asset-right approach. The segment disclosure shows sale of properties held for sale of S$50.655 million against cost of properties sold of S$48.256 million: a gross profit of S$2.399 million, or 4.74 per cent. The Development segment’s S$4.176 million of total operating profit came mostly from its S$3.386 million share of associates, not from the sale.

The disposals also explain the cash flow. Net cash from operating activities of S$64.9 million against S$17.2 million looks transformational, but S$48.255 million of it is the working capital release as properties held for sale left the balance sheet. Excluding that, operating cash flow was approximately S$16.7 million, against S$17.2 million — effectively unchanged. Westminster Fire Station alone is disclosed as approximately 19 per cent of group revenue, or roughly S$47.2 million.

There is no revaluation in this result at all

Notes 9 and 10 record that the Group assessed the 31 December 2025 external valuations as remaining appropriate at 30 June 2026, and that accordingly no fair value movements were recognised on investment properties or on property, plant and equipment for the six months. Investment properties moved from S$1,030.5 million to S$1,027.2 million on S$1.5 million of additions and S$4.8 million of adverse currency translation, and nothing else.

Every dollar of the S$11.6 million profit after tax is therefore operating, equity-accounted or transactional in origin. That is worth stating plainly, because it is not the norm this season. Across the Singapore developer cohort this reporting season, half-year profits have frequently carried material fair value movements. This one does not.

Strip the one-offs and the half improved

Profit before income tax fell 28.1 per cent, profit after tax 35.6 per cent, profit attributable to equity holders 39.9 per cent, and earnings per share from 4.01 cents to 2.40 cents. The dominant cause is disclosed and singular: a one-off non-cash gain of S$9.1 million recognised in the comparative period when the Group acquired an additional 6.7 per cent of Woodlands Square Pte. Ltd. for S$25.0 million at a consideration below the fair value of the net assets acquired.

Removing other gains from both periods gives a pre-tax result of S$14.057 million against S$12.427 million, an increase of 13.1 per cent. The Group’s own adjusted measures point the same way — adjusted PATMI of S$9.8 million against S$9.1 million, up 7.7 per cent. It is also worth noting what the current period’s S$2.554 million of other gains consists of: 96.9 per cent of it is an unrealised currency translation gain of S$2.476 million, driven by a stronger Australian dollar. The adjusted figures are the honest ones here, and the company leads on them.

The tax line is the one nobody explains

Income tax expense of S$4.994 million against profit before tax of S$16.611 million is an effective rate of 30.1 per cent, up from 21.9 per cent. Because the share of results of associates and joint ventures is already post-tax, the rate on the remainder is higher still: 37.1 per cent. The Singapore headline corporate rate is 17 per cent.

A shift of geography toward the United Kingdom and Australia, non-deductible amortisation on the HFS intangibles, and losses not available for relief would each move the rate in that direction. Neither the media release nor the interim financial statements says which, or in what proportion. The tax note discloses the split between current and deferred tax and an under-provision of S$148,000 relating to prior years, and stops there. It is the single largest unexplained movement in the accounts.

The owned portfolio is going backwards while the platform grows

Revenue from the Group’s owned PBSA portfolio fell 4.41 per cent to S$29.0 million, and portfolio occupancy for the academic year that began in September 2025 was approximately 88 per cent against 92 per cent the year before. Total operating profit from student accommodation property ownership fell 4.91 per cent to S$14.6 million. That segment, not the management platform, remains where the money is: S$14.6 million against S$1.7 million.

Targeted refurbishment is under way across selected properties, due to complete before the coming academic year. Pre-leasing for AY26/27 is described as tracking marginally ahead of the same point last year, with rental growth moderated. UCAS reported a record 665,070 applicants, up 4.6 per cent, with international applicants up 7.1 per cent. Demand for beds is not the question. Pricing and city-level competition are.

Gross deleveraging outran net deleveraging

Total borrowings fell to S$590.0 million from S$647.3 million and the debt-to-equity ratio from 45.4 per cent to 41.2 per cent, both of which reconcile exactly to the balance sheet. Cash, however, fell by S$32.6 million to S$146.2 million, so on a net basis the improvement is smaller: net borrowings to total equity moved from 32.9 per cent to 31.0 per cent, a 1.9 point improvement against the 4.3 points on the gross measure. Average cost of debt fell to 4.9 per cent from 5.2 per cent, 64.0 per cent of borrowings are fixed, and a July refinancing of approximately S$61 million lifted average debt maturity to roughly 4.0 years.

One smaller item sits in the equity statement. Under the scrip dividend scheme for the FY2025 dividend, 1,041,507 new shares were issued for S$1.285 million — an issue price of S$1.2338 per share against net asset value per share of S$2.877, or 0.43 times book. The comparable FY2024 issue was at S$1.0030. The quantum is small, approximately S$1.71 million of net asset value, but the mechanism is not: scrip settled below book is dilutive to net asset value per share every time it runs, and it runs annually.

The related-party ring, and a fee disclosure worth reading

Far East Orchard leases hotels from Far East Hospitality Trust, manages hotels for entities associated with its controlling shareholders, and held a 33 per cent stake in the trust’s manager. Interested person transactions under the shareholders’ mandate totalled S$20.325 million for the half, with a further S$108,000 outside it. Of the Group’s external hospitality management revenue, 70.8 per cent came from interested persons. Rental expense to Far East Hospitality Trust for hotels was S$7.025 million.

The footnote to that table is the most informative disclosure in either document. FEO Hospitality Asset Management Pte. Ltd., manager of Far East Hospitality Trust, is entitled to a base fee of 0.28 per cent per annum of deposited property and a performance fee of 4.0 per cent per annum of net property income or the annual distributable amount, whichever is lower. The S$1.695 million recorded is stated to be 33 per cent of the fees received in the period, being the value at risk to the Group — which implies total management fees of approximately S$5.136 million for the half. The footnote says the Company “was” a 33 per cent shareholder during the period. The past tense is not explained.

A court ruling after the balance sheet date

Far East Opus Pte. Ltd., a joint venture in which the Group holds 20 per cent, has been defending claims brought by unit owners of SBF Center. On 20 July 2026, after the balance sheet date, the Court of Appeal allowed the appeal by the claimant in the test case, and all six remaining claims will now proceed to trial. The Group states that its maximum exposure is limited to its proportional 20 per cent share and is not expected to be material, and no provision has been made. The direction of travel since the last report is nonetheless adverse.

What to watch

Three things will determine whether FEOR30 reads as a platform build or as revenue without margin. Whether the student accommodation management segment’s margin rises once the purchase price allocation amortisation is understood as a fixed multi-year charge against a roughly S$88.5 million amortisable intangible base. Whether AY26/27 occupancy recovers the four points lost, given that pre-leasing is ahead but pricing is not. And whether the effective tax rate settles nearer 30 per cent, which on this profit base is worth more than the entire student accommodation management segment.
