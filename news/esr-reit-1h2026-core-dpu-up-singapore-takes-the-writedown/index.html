Core DPU Up 4.5%, Singapore Down S$54.3 Million — ESR-REIT Sells Ten Short-Lease Assets at a Premium and Buys Freehold Melbourne

ESR-REIT’s first half reads better than the portfolio behind it. Core distribution per unit rose 4.5% to 11.250 cents and aggregate leverage fell to 41.4%, but the entire S$54.3 million of fair-value loss landed on the Singapore book. The manager’s answer…

By PropertyAtlas·28 July 2026·8 min read
Photo: 12 Ang Mo Kio Street 65, Singapore. Credit: ESR-REIT.

ESR-REIT reported results for the six months to 30 June 2026 on 28 July 2026, and the distribution line is the one every wire will carry. Core distribution per unit came in at 11.250 cents, up 4.5% year-on-year, with total DPU of 11.510 cents up 2.4%. Aggregate leverage fell from 43.4% to 41.4%, the MAS interest coverage ratio improved to 2.6 times, and Fitch has the trust at ‘BBB’ with a stable outlook. On the reported metrics this is a good half.

The portfolio underneath tells a more specific story. Gross revenue fell 0.3% to S$222.3 million and net property income fell 2.2% to S$162.7 million, both a function of having sold ten properties. Strip those out and the same-store book grew — revenue up 2.3%, net property income up 0.7%. The gap between the reported decline and the same-store increase is the whole strategy in one line: ESR-REIT is shrinking deliberately in Singapore and rebuilding elsewhere.

The writedown has one address

Investment properties were written down by S$56.4 million over the half, comprising a S$48.1 million valuation loss and S$8.3 million of straight-line rent and marketing commission adjustments. The geographic split is the part worth pausing on: Singapore −S$54.3 million, Australia −S$2.3 million, Japan +S$0.2 million.

Capitalisation rates did not move — the range held at 3.80% to 7.75%. So this is not a repricing of the market. With cap rates flat, a valuation loss concentrated in a single geography points at the denominator that shrinks on its own: remaining land lease. The Singapore book carries a weighted average land lease of 31.2 years, and every six months that number falls by six months unless something is bought, sold or topped up. Net asset value per unit fell from S$2.55 to S$2.50 over the same period, and the manager attributes the decline to precisely this fair-value loss.

Selling short, buying freehold

Across FY2025 and the first half of 2026, ESR-REIT completed the divestment of ten non-core Singapore properties. Nine of them completed in this half, generating S$439.1 million of disposal proceeds. Eight sold as a portfolio in June for an aggregate S$338.1 million at a 2.0% premium to valuation, with a weighted average remaining land lease of 21.9 years. The hotel strata lot at ESR BizPark @ Changi went separately in March for S$101.0 million, at valuation.

Selling 21.9-year leasehold at a 2.0% premium is a good outcome. What the proceeds bought is the more consequential half of the trade.

In July, after the balance sheet date, the manager announced the acquisition of six freehold logistics properties in Melbourne — five under a 7 July agreement and a sixth, 18 Foxley Court in Derrimut, added on 23 July. Headline purchase consideration is A$341.1 million, about S$305.4 million at the illustrative rate of A$1.00 to S$0.89534.1 The assets total 145,640 square metres of gross lettable area, are around ten years old, are 92.3% occupied and are let to eight tenants including CEVA Logistics, Silk Logistics, Nick Scali and HealthShare Victoria. The manager puts first-year net property income yield on purchase consideration at 5.6% and expects the transaction to be 5.1% accretive to DPU on a pro forma basis, taking distribution per unit from 20.091 cents to 21.109 cents after the divestments.

The six assets are 15 & 33 Archer Road and 4–12 Doriemus Drive in Truganina; 64 West Park Drive and 18 Foxley Court in Derrimut; and 39 Naxos Way and 58–76 Naxos Way & 68 Atlantic Drive in Keysborough.

Two details do more work than the headline. In-place rents across the six are estimated at 12% to 17% below prevailing market, on Colliers’ first-quarter reading of the Australian industrial market — which means the reversion upside is contracted-in rather than hoped-for, with a portfolio weighted average lease expiry of 3.5 years and 48% of leases expiring in 2030. And 18 Foxley Court, the late addition, was acquired at around 9.0% below replacement cost on a JLL valuation. Buying modern logistics below the cost of building it is the cleanest single fact in the release.

The effect on portfolio shape is measurable. Singapore falls from 82.2% of rental income to 78.8% on a post-transaction basis, against 83.7% a year ago. Freehold assets rise from 19.8% of portfolio valuation to 25.1%. Weighted average land lease expiry lengthens from 44.7 years to 48.4 years.

Read that last number carefully. The portfolio average lengthens by 3.7 years. Singapore’s own average moves from 31.2 years to 31.3 — a tenth of a year. The land lease decay the manager describes as having been largely addressed has been addressed by adding freehold Australia to the denominator, not by extending anything in Singapore. That is a legitimate strategy, and arguably the only one available to a Singapore industrial REIT holding 30-year statutory-board tenures. It is dilution rather than repair, and the two are worth distinguishing.

The one number no wire will carry

Alongside the results, ESR-REIT announced the divestment of 12 Ang Mo Kio Street 65. The contract was entered on 21 July 2026 at a sale consideration of S$33.3 million, a 2.1% premium to the S$32.6 million independent valuation by Edmund Tie & Company (SEA) Pte Ltd as at 30 June 2026. The asset is a high-specifications industrial building of 16,754 square metres gross floor area, zoned Business 1 under Master Plan 2025, around 30 years old, 91.8% occupied, with roughly 24 years of land lease remaining.2 Completion is expected in the third quarter of 2026, at which point the portfolio falls from 62 properties to 61.

Divide the consideration by the floor area and the transaction prices at approximately S$1,987 per square metre, or about S$185 per square foot of gross floor area. Neither the announcement nor the presentation states it. It is a straightforward derivation, and it is the number that matters to anyone pricing comparable high-specification industrial stock in Ang Mo Kio with a similar lease tail. It will surface in URA REALIS on completion in the third quarter, at which point PropertyAtlas will pair it against the building’s own transaction history.

For context on what a 24-year tail costs: the eight-property June portfolio sold at a weighted average 21.9 years of remaining land lease and still cleared a 2.0% premium to valuation. The short-lease Singapore industrial bid is not weak. It is pricing a wasting asset honestly.

Operationally, the book is tightening

Rental reversion came in at +9.8% for the half, marginally ahead of the +9.7% recorded a year earlier. Portfolio occupancy was 91.9%, up from 91.2%, with Singapore at 89.9% against a JTC first-quarter 2026 industrial average of 88.9%. Retention was 83.7%, weighted average lease expiry lengthened to 4.8 years from 4.1, and rental collections ran at about 97.4% of receivables. Some 260,976 square metres were leased over the half, roughly two-thirds of it renewals.

The segment split behind the +9.8% is where the texture sits: logistics +11.3%, general industrial +8.4%, high-specifications industrial +5.0%, business park +0.5%. Business park is effectively flat, and it is 14.2% of rental income. The strength of the aggregate number is a logistics story, which is consistent with where the manager is putting capital.

Two asset enhancement projects completed. 16 Tai Seng Street obtained temporary occupation permit in July 2025 at a cost of about S$33.0 million and an illustrative 6.0% yield on cost, but is only around 53% occupied as at the second quarter, with negotiations underway that would take it to about 68% in the second half. 29 Tai Seng Street obtained TOP on 18 June 2026 — a conversion from general industrial to high-specifications industrial at a cost of about S$6.8 million and a 6.4% illustrative yield, with the manager in advanced negotiations on all remaining space. Its valuation moved from S$32.9 million to S$34.7 million.

The next commitment is a big one

The item most likely to be overlooked sits in the presentation rather than the financials. 2 Fishery Port Road — the decommissioned Cold Centre, currently carried at S$38.7 million as property under redevelopment — is planned for redevelopment as a cold storage and food processing facility. Estimated start is the fourth quarter of 2026, with a construction period of up to about 30 months and an estimated cost of S$200 million to S$250 million, against an illustrative stabilised yield on cost of about 7.0% and an equity internal rate of return of 12% to 15%.

That is a commitment approaching the size of the entire Melbourne acquisition, on a single Singapore site, with completion in 2029. It sits at planning stage and no capital commitment has been recognised beyond S$2.8 million of redevelopment and asset enhancement spend, so it is not yet a balance sheet event. It is, however, the clearest signal of where the manager sees Singapore value — not in holding short-lease general industrial, but in rebuilding specialised logistics on land it already controls.

Capital structure: better ratios, more expensive debt

Gearing at 41.4% is guided down to 39.9% once S$125 million of unsecured notes maturing in August 2026 are redeemed from divestment proceeds. Interest coverage is 2.6 times against a 1.5 times regulatory minimum. Debt headroom is S$871.4 million, with about S$284 million of committed undrawn revolving credit facilities across ten lending banks.

The cost of debt moved the other way, rising to 3.52% from 3.35%. The manager’s explanation is mechanical and credible — divestment proceeds were used to pay down cheap revolving credit facilities first, leaving a more expensive residual mix. Fixed-rate exposure is 75.5%, but the weighted average fixed debt expiry shortened to 1.41 years from 1.9, which means the hedge book rolls off sooner than the debt does. The manager expects loan margin compression of around 30 basis points on upcoming refinancing on the strength of the Fitch rating.

One line deserves stating plainly, because it appears twice in the financial statements: net current liabilities stand at S$764.1 million at group level and S$227.5 million at trust level, driven by S$833.6 million of borrowings falling due within twelve months. The manager states it expects to refinance and to meet its obligations as they fall due, and the undrawn facilities, debt headroom and investment-grade rating support that. It is a normal position for a REIT of this size and it is not a distress signal, but it is the reason the August notes redemption and the pace of refinancing are worth watching.

A separate detail is worth noting for anyone modelling operating expenses. The manager has entered three-year fixed-rate energy contracts covering 23 of its 26 multi-tenanted Singapore properties with effect from the fourth quarter of 2026, explicitly to hedge energy price volatility arising from the ongoing Middle East conflict. Property expenses rose 5.3% this half, and utilities and property tax were cited as the offset to same-store net property income growth. That hedge is a direct response.

What to watch

  • Completion of the Melbourne six, and the point at which Singapore drops below 80% of rental income.
  • 12 Ang Mo Kio Street 65 completing in the third quarter, and the caveat surfacing in URA REALIS at around S$185 per square foot of gross floor area.
  • Whether 2 Fishery Port Road moves from planning to committed capital in the fourth quarter, and how a S$200 million to S$250 million Singapore redevelopment is funded alongside an offshore acquisition programme.
  • The August redemption of S$125 million of notes and the path from 41.4% gearing to the guided 39.9%.
  • Lease-up at 16 Tai Seng Street, still only around 53% occupied a year after TOP.
  • S$268.9 million of the 2023 equity fundraising proceeds remaining unutilised three years on, against S$299.7 million raised.
