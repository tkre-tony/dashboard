Revenue −4.5%, NPI −6.0% And DPU −45.5% Year On Year, But Flat To Slightly Ahead Half On Half. S$493,000 Of A S$3.630 Million Return Reached Unitholders; S$3.137 Million Went To Non-Controlling Interests.

The REIT reported a half of falling revenue and a halved distribution. Underneath the headline figures, S$493,000 of a S$3.630 million return reached Unitholders — and the portfolio every disclosure is measured against is a quarter larger than the one the…

By PropertyAtlas·9 August 2026·9 min read
Photo: Beijing Wanliu Mall, No. 2 Bagou Road, Haidian District, Beijing — BHG Retail REIT’s largest asset by net property income, held at a 60 per cent interest. Photo: BHG Retail REIT

BHG Retail REIT reported gross revenue of S$26.837 million for the six months to 30 June 2026, down 4.5 per cent, with net property income of S$14.099 million, down 6.0 per cent, and a distribution per unit of 0.12 Singapore cents against 0.22 cents a year earlier. Of the S$3.630 million total return after taxation, S$493,000 was attributable to Unitholders and S$3.137 million to non-controlling interests — the 40 per cent of Beijing Wanliu that the REIT does not own.

Unitholders took 13.6 per cent of the group’s return, down from 18.3 per cent in the corresponding half. The cash movement is starker: dividends paid to non-controlling interests during the period were S$3.643 million, against S$364,000 distributed to Unitholders. Ten to one.

Where the Income Sits

Beijing Wanliu generated S$10.106 million of net property income in the half, 71.7 per cent of the portfolio total, on a 60.6 per cent NPI margin. Together with Chengdu Konggang it accounts for 91.2 per cent of portfolio net property income. The REIT owns all of Chengdu Konggang and 60 per cent of Beijing Wanliu, so the asset that dominates the accounts is the one where the entitlement is partial.

The same gap runs through the valuation. The portfolio is appraised at RMB 4,694.0 million, a figure that consolidates Beijing Wanliu at 100 per cent. The REIT’s own footnote puts its 60 per cent share of that asset at RMB 1,504.2 million. Substituting the owned interest for the consolidated one gives a look-through portfolio of RMB 3,691.2 million. The headline is 27.2 per cent larger than the economic position behind it.

The REIT listed on the Main Board in December 2015 as the first pure-play China retail REIT sponsored by a mainland retail group. It holds six community malls across 179,748 square metres of net lettable area, with 519,603,000 units in issue, unchanged through the period. The sponsor is Beijing Hualian Department Store, listed in Shenzhen.

Sequentially, the Decline Has Flattened

A year-on-year read overstates what happened in this particular half. FY2025 was itself a heavy year: revenue fell 9.6 per cent to S$55.106 million and net property income 11.6 per cent to S$29.030 million, and the REIT booked a S$7.235 million reduction in the fair value of its investment properties on top of a S$4.798 million reduction the year before.

Deriving the second half of 2025 from the full year gives revenue of S$27.017 million and net property income of S$14.030 million. Against that base, the half just reported was down 0.7 per cent on revenue and up 0.5 per cent on net property income. The decline against 1H 2025 is real, but the comparator was the strongest half of a declining series.

Occupancy tells the same story. The committed rate ran 95.8 per cent at December 2024, 95.1 per cent at June 2025 and 93.4 per cent at December 2025. At 30 June 2026 it was 94.0 per cent — the first rise in the series. Both Hefei malls improved: Mengchenglu from 89.9 to 90.3 per cent, Changjiangxilu from 78.4 to 79.9 per cent.

Occupancy Is Measuring the Wrong Thing at Two Assets

Two of the six assets are stated at 100.0 per cent occupancy: Xining Huayuan and Dalian Jinsanjiao, each with a weighted average lease expiry of 8.5 years by net lettable area against 2.4 years at Beijing Wanliu.

Both are master-leased. Both received rental support from the REIT during the period, which the manager cites as one of two reasons net property income fell. Xining’s revenue dropped 26.7 per cent to S$822,000 and Dalian’s 39.4 per cent to S$576,000. Their combined revenue fell 32.6 per cent.

An occupancy figure at a master-leased asset describes the master lease, not the trading underneath it. Stating 100.0 per cent alongside a 39.4 per cent revenue decline, with landlord support flowing to the lessee, is accurate and uninformative in the same line.

Two Malls That Produce Almost No Net Income

Hefei Mengchenglu returned S$5,000 of net property income on S$1.239 million of revenue in the half — a margin of 0.4 per cent. Hefei Changjiangxilu returned S$125,000 on S$1.647 million, a margin of 7.6 per cent. Net property income fell 66.7 per cent and 70.8 per cent respectively.

The two assets carry 52,853 square metres of net lettable area between them, 29.4 per cent of the portfolio. They contributed S$130,000, or 0.9 per cent of net property income.

The manager attributes the occupancy at both to ongoing tenancy rejuvenation, and the results presentation shows new leisure, children’s and electronics tenants at each. That accounts for the occupancy. The interim filing does not break out property operating expenses by asset, so it does not account for a 0.4 per cent margin.

What “Experiential” Counts

The manager frames the leasing strategy around experiential retail, stating that close to 65 per cent of gross rental income and 67 per cent of net lettable area now come from the experiential segment. The bracket beside that claim defines it: the figure excludes fashion and accessories.

The arithmetic is exact. Fashion and accessories account for 34.7 per cent of gross rental income and 33.2 per cent of net lettable area, leaving 65.3 and 66.8 per cent. Everything else counts as experiential, including the supermarket category at 6.4 per cent of area and food and beverage at 22.5 per cent. The measure covers the four multi-tenanted malls only.

That is a broad definition. It is disclosed rather than buried, and the underlying leasing is real — the half brought a drone retailer and a 3D printing brand to Beijing Wanliu, a dinosaur-themed attraction to Hefei Changjiangxilu and a themed restaurant to Hefei Mengchenglu. But a figure that counts a supermarket as an experience is measuring the absence of fashion.

A Balance Sheet That Improved Because the Renminbi Did

Net asset value per unit rose to S$0.73 from S$0.68, an increase of 7.4 per cent. Unitholders’ funds rose S$24.063 million over the six months. S$23.878 million of that — 99.2 per cent — is the foreign currency translation reserve.

Investment properties rose to S$892.813 million from S$859.162 million. The movement comprises S$211,000 of additions, a S$348,000 write-off, no change in fair value, and S$33.788 million of translation differences. The properties were not revalued for interim reporting; the RMB figures are unchanged from Knight Frank Petty’s 31 December 2025 valuation, and the manager states it believes the carrying amount reflects fair value at 30 June.

Gearing improved to 40.1 per cent from 41.6 per cent on the same mechanism. Loans and borrowings were S$299.948 million against S$300.415 million at the year end, a movement of 0.2 per cent, while total assets rose 3.3 per cent. The ratio fell because the denominator grew.

The Coverage Ratio Is the Constraint

Interest coverage was 1.7 times, unchanged from the year end, and the manager notes it sits below 1.8 times while remaining above the 1.5 times regulatory minimum under the CIS Code. The filing discloses the two sensitivity scenarios prescribed by the Monetary Authority of Singapore: a 10 per cent fall in EBITDA takes coverage to 1.6 times, and a one percentage point rise in the weighted average interest rate takes it to 1.4 times.

The second sits below the regulatory floor. It is a prescribed hypothetical rather than a forecast, and disclosing it is a requirement rather than a warning. It is also the only figure in the set showing what a modest move in rates would do to the position.

Against that, the financing has been managed down. Finance costs fell 9.7 per cent to S$7.348 million. Average cost of debt was 4.0 per cent, or 4.7 per cent including amortisation of loan establishment fees, against 4.2 per cent for FY2025. Interest rate swaps rose to a notional S$123.0 million from S$82.0 million at an average 2.00 per cent against 2.30 per cent, covering about 40 per cent of borrowings. Weighted average term to maturity was 1.8 years, down from 2.3 years at the year end, with facilities maturing in March 2028.

A Distribution Funded From Capital

The 0.12 cent distribution is declared entirely as a capital distribution, representing a return of capital for Singapore income tax purposes. The filing states it is expected to be funded from debt and/or internal cash flow from operations. Books close at 5.00pm on 20 August 2026 and payment falls on 28 September 2026.

The amount to be distributed was S$623,000 against S$1.148 million, down 45.7 per cent, with approximately S$0.1 million retained for operating expenses and working capital. Basic earnings per unit was 0.09 cents. The distribution exceeds the earnings behind it, which is what a capital distribution is.

The Fee Stack

The manager’s base fee fell 40.8 per cent to S$84,000, tracking the distributable income it is calculated on at 10 per cent per annum. The trustee’s fee was S$71,000.

A performance fee of S$61,000 was accrued, against nil in the corresponding half. Under the trust deed the performance fee is 25 per cent of the difference in distribution per unit in a financial year against the preceding financial year, multiplied by weighted average units, and the FY2025 annual report states it is payable only if DPU in a financial year exceeds DPU in the preceding financial year, payable annually.

The distribution series runs 0.50 cents for FY2024, 0.29 cents for FY2025 and 0.12 cents for the half just reported. No performance fee was payable for FY2025, consistent with the fall. For a fee to become payable for FY2026, the full-year distribution would need to exceed 0.29 cents, which would require a second half above 0.17 cents against a second half of 2025 that delivered 0.07 cents. The filing does not explain the basis of the accrual.

The larger number in the fee stack is not the manager’s. Property management fees and reimbursables were S$1.949 million for FY2025 against a manager’s base fee of S$182,000 and no performance fee — a ratio of nearly eleven to one. The property manager is BHG Mall (Singapore) Property Management, a sponsor-related entity, entitled to 2 per cent of gross revenue, 2.5 per cent of net property income and a two-month lease-up commission on new tenancies of three years or more. The interim statements fold that fee into property operating expenses and do not disclose it separately.

What the Half Leaves

The land tenure is finite and shortening. Remaining lease terms run from 22 years at Xining Huayuan to 16 years at Dalian Jinsanjiao, the asset written down 8.2 per cent in the last annual valuation. Beijing Wanliu, which carries the portfolio, has 18 years to 2044.

The manager has said it will continue to explore acquisitions from the sponsor’s pipeline and third parties. On the current balance sheet, with coverage at 1.7 times and gearing at 40.1 per cent, that is a statement of intent rather than near-term capacity.

What this half shows is a portfolio that has stopped falling half on half and a distribution that has not. Revenue is flat against the preceding half, net property income slightly ahead of it, and occupancy up for the first time in two years. The distribution is down 45.5 per cent year on year, funded from capital, and the return reaching Unitholders is a seventh of what the accounts report — because the accounts report an asset base a quarter larger than the one the REIT owns.
