United Hampshire US REIT Has Agreed To Sell A Single-Tenant BJ's Wholesale Club In Massachusetts For US$34.0 Million — The Rationale Measures The Leverage Improvement Off A 31st December 2025 Base The Trust Superseded Two Days Before The Announcement

By PropertyAtlas·20 August 2026·9 min read
Illustration: PropertyAtlas. United Hampshire US REIT agreed on 17th August 2026 to sell BJ's Quincy for US$34.0 million, a premium of 0.9 per cent over book value, 4.9 per cent over the 1st July 2026 independent valuation and 1.2 per cent over the original purchase price, against an estimated loss on divestment of about US$0.4 million after transaction costs. Source: UHREIT, Proposed Divestment of BJ's Quincy, released to SGX 18th August 2026.

United Hampshire US REIT announced on 18th August 2026 that its indirectly wholly owned subsidiary BJ'S Quincy 2016 LLC has entered into a conditional purchase and sale agreement with 200 Crown, LLC, an unrelated third party, to sell BJ's Quincy for an aggregate consideration of US$34.0 million. The property is a single-storey freehold building of 84,360 square feet at 200 Crown Colony Drive, Quincy, Massachusetts, completed in 2009 and let to BJ's Wholesale Club. Completion is expected on 15th September 2026. The agreement was entered into on 17th August, United States time.

That is a routine disposal of one asset from a portfolio of twenty-three. What makes it worth reading closely is the arithmetic in the paragraphs around it.

The Improvement Is Measured Off A Base The Trust Superseded Two Days Earlier

The rationale section states that the divestment is expected to lower UHREIT’s aggregate leverage ratio from 38.6 per cent as at 31st December 2025 to 35.9 per cent on a pro forma basis, and to improve its adjusted interest coverage ratio from 2.4 times for FY2025 to 2.5 times.

Every one of those figures is drawn from the audited FY2025 financial statements, and the announcement says so plainly and repeatedly. The pro forma tables carry a bolded illustrative-purposes-only caveat and state the assumed completion dates. Nothing is concealed.

The point is what sits beside it. PropertyAtlas reported on 16th August 2026 — two days before this announcement — that aggregate leverage as defined in the Property Funds Appendix had risen from 38.6 per cent to 40.4 per cent at 30th June 2026. In the same reporting we noted the revolving credit facility drawn from US$9.0 million to US$29.5 million, and the proportion of borrowings at fixed rates or hedged to fixed falling from 76.2 per cent to 71.5 per cent.

So the trust published a 40.4 per cent number on Sunday and a rationale built on a 38.6 per cent base on Tuesday.

Both are defensible in isolation. Pro forma effects are conventionally struck on the last audited accounts, and a half-year position is not audited. But the pro forma tables are one thing and the rationale prose is another: the rationale is where a reader forms an impression of what the sale achieves, and there the 38.6 per cent figure appears without the qualifier that the trust’s own most recent disclosure puts leverage 1.8 percentage points higher.

Applied to the more recent base, the direction of the improvement is unchanged and the destination is not 35.9 per cent.

A Premium On Three Measures, And A Loss

The consideration is described as a premium three times in a single paragraph.

It is US$0.3 million, or 0.9 per cent, above book value. It is US$1.6 million, or 4.9 per cent, above the independent valuation of US$32.4 million conducted by Cushman & Wakefield as at 1st July 2026 and commissioned by the trustee. And it is US$0.4 million, or 1.2 per cent, higher than the US$33.6 million purchase price.

All three recompute exactly.

The next sentence discloses an estimated loss on divestment based on book value of approximately US$0.4 million, after transaction-related expenses of approximately US$0.7 million.

There is no contradiction, and the arithmetic is worth setting out because it explains the whole transaction in one line: a premium over book of US$0.3 million, less US$0.7 million of costs, is a loss of US$0.4 million. The premium is smaller than the cost of realising it.

Two further things follow from the same set of figures, neither of them stated.

The first is that the independent valuer marked the asset below what UHREIT paid for it. The valuation of US$32.4 million as at 1st July 2026 sits US$1.2 million under the US$33.6 million purchase price. The trust sold above that valuation, which is the disclosed comparison; it also sold only 1.2 per cent above cost, across the entire holding period.

The second is the per-square-foot progression. On 84,360 square feet, the purchase price was about US$398 per square foot, the July 2026 valuation about US$384, and the agreed consideration about US$403. A tight range for a freehold single-tenant box, and a reminder that the headline “premium” language is doing work across three quite different reference points.

The Asset Is 7.6 Per Cent Of NAV And 4.1 Per Cent Of Net Property Income

This is the part that explains why the trust is selling, and it comes from UHREIT’s own Rule 1006 disclosure rather than from any assumption of ours.

For the purposes of classifying the transaction under Chapter 10 of the Listing Manual, the announcement discloses that the property’s net asset value is 7.6 per cent of the trust’s NAV, while the net property income attributable to it is 4.1 per cent of the trust’s FY2025 net property income.

An asset carrying 7.6 per cent of the balance sheet and producing 4.1 per cent of the income is yielding a little over half the portfolio average against what it occupies. On the disclosed absolutes, the property produced US$1.991 million of net property income against a book value of US$33.7 million — about 5.9 per cent — and against the agreed consideration, an exit yield of about 5.9 per cent.

That is a low-yielding asset by the standards of the book it sits in, and selling it is exactly what a capital recycling strategy should do.

The Pro Forma Says What The Proceeds Are Retiring

Here the disclosure is unusually informative, though not deliberately.

The pro forma DPU table shows net property income falling from US$48.951 million to US$46.960 million — a reduction of US$1.991 million, which ties exactly to the property’s contribution. Income available for distribution falls from US$26.382 million to US$26.380 million — a reduction of US$2,000.

Losing US$1.99 million of property income costs the trust US$2,000 of distributable income. The implied offsetting benefit is therefore about US$1.989 million a year, against estimated net proceeds of US$33.3 million.

If that benefit is substantially interest saved on borrowings repaid — which is what the rationale says the proceeds will do — it implies a cost of roughly 6.0 per cent on the debt being retired. That is well above the 4.89 per cent trailing cost of debt the trust headlined at its 1H 2026 results, and consistent with repaying the revolving credit facility, which is floating and would sit above the blended average, rather than paying down term debt.

We would not state the implied rate as a disclosed figure. The offset may also include a smaller management fee on a smaller asset base. But the direction is not in doubt, and the structural point survives either reading: the trust is selling an asset yielding about 5.9 per cent to retire borrowings costing about 6 per cent. That is why distribution per unit is unchanged at 4.39 US cents rather than falling, and it is a better argument for the transaction than the leverage sentence the rationale actually leads with.

Net asset value falls by US$200,000, and NAV per unit is US$0.73 before and after — the movement is real but below the rounding.

What Completion Actually Depends On

The conditions precedent are worth naming, because one of them puts the outcome partly in the hands of the tenant.

Completion requires title to be insurable, and it requires BJ’s Wholesale Club, Inc. — the anchor and sole tenant — to deliver an executed estoppel certificate conforming in all material respects to the prescribed form and disclosing no material matters.

An estoppel certificate is standard in United States single-tenant sales, and there is no suggestion of difficulty. It is simply worth registering that a sale of a single-tenant asset carries a condition the tenant has to satisfy.

The purchaser has placed US$650,000 in escrow, about 1.9 per cent of the consideration, non-refundable except on seller default.

The trust also states the divestment is in the ordinary course of business and therefore not subject to unitholder approval under Rule 1014(3).

A Note On The Rule 1006 Wording

The three relative figures disclosed are 7.6 per cent on net asset value, 4.1 per cent on net property income and 10.6 per cent on consideration against market capitalisation. All three recompute from the absolutes given.

The announcement then states that all of the relative figures exceed 5.0 per cent but do not exceed 20.0 per cent, and classifies the divestment as a disclosable transaction.

The classification is correct. Rule 1010 requires only that one of the figures fall in that band, and two of them comfortably do. But 4.1 per cent does not exceed 5.0 per cent, so the sentence as drafted does not describe the table above it. It is a drafting slip in a boilerplate paragraph, with no consequence for the outcome, and it is noted here only because the figures in that table are the ones a reader would use.

A related detail: the market capitalisation of US$319.781 million is computed on a weighted average unit price of US$0.5258 on 14th August 2026. That implies about 608.2 million units in issue, against the 605.463 million used in every pro forma table in the same document, which are stated as at 31st December 2025. Both are labelled. They are simply from different dates, and the Rule 1006(c) figure is the only one in the announcement using a current unit count.

What This Is And What It Is Not

This is a competent disposal of the least productive asset in the portfolio, at a price above an independent valuation, into a use of proceeds the trust has flagged as debt reduction. On the arithmetic, it is accretive for a reason the announcement never states.

What it is not is a transaction that returns leverage to 35.9 per cent, because leverage was not 38.6 per cent when the agreement was signed. The proceeds are real and the direction is right. The destination is further away than the rationale implies, and the trust’s own results, published two days earlier, are where a reader would find that out.

The use of proceeds is stated in the widest available terms — repaying existing debts, financing capital expenditure, funding potential acquisitions, or other general corporate requirements. No redeployment is named.
