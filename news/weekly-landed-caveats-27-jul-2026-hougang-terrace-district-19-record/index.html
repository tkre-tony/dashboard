Weekly Landed Caveats, Week of 27 July 2026: 45 Landed Transactions Worth S$279.6 Million — A Hougang Terrace Sets a District 19 Freehold Record at S$3,921 Psf Measured Against 6,524 Prior Sales

Forty-five landed caveats across the 28 and 31 July releases, totalling S$279.6 million. A terrace off Simon Lane cleared S$3,921 per square foot, the highest ever paid for a freehold terrace house in District 19 and a mark measured against 6,524 prior…

By PropertyAtlas·1 August 2026·6 min read
Photo: Illustration: PropertyAtlas. The 17 July sale off Simon Lane at S$3,921 per square foot is the highest ever paid for a freehold terrace house in District 19, measured against 6,524 prior sales. Source: URA REALIS, releases dated 28 and 31 July 2026.

Forty-five landed caveats were lodged across the 28 and 31 July URA releases, totalling S$279.6 million. Thirty matched to a prior sale at the same address, producing 28 gains and two losses. Those thirty sellers had paid S$105.7 million for their houses; they sold them for S$193.1 million.

Two release dates, Tuesday 28 and Friday 31 July, are reported here as one week. Sale dates span 11 February to 24 July 2026 — caveat lodgement lags the transaction, and one February straggler arrived in the Friday drop. Sixteen districts are represented, and only three of the forty-five are strata title; the rest hold land.

Price per square foot is reported separately for freehold and leasehold, because the two are not comparable. Freehold and 999-year titles accounted for 38 of the 45 caveats, ranging from S$972 to S$3,945 per square foot and averaging S$2,235. The seven leasehold transactions ranged from S$189 to S$2,622, averaging S$1,525. The week’s lowest figure, S$189 per square foot, is a house on a 70-year lease granted in 1964 with roughly eight years left to run.

A District 19 record, measured against 6,524 sales

A freehold terrace house off Simon Lane sold on 17 July for S$6.28 million on 1,602 square feet of land — S$3,921 per square foot, the highest ever paid for a freehold terrace house in District 19. The previous best was S$3,754.

Records here are measured like against like. A house is compared only with others of the same type, the same tenure and the same district, so this sale is measured against the 6,524 freehold terraces that have sold in District 19 since records began — not against semi-detached houses, leasehold stock, or other parts of the island. District 19 freehold terrace is the most heavily traded grain in the landed market, and 6,524 is the deepest denominator anywhere in the dataset. This is not a record set on a thin sample.

A second district record fell in the same release. A freehold semi-detached house on Phoenix Walk sold at S$8.00 million against a District 23 prior high of S$7.80 million, across 491 prior transactions. That one is a price record rather than a rate record: at S$2,273 per square foot it sits well inside the district’s range.

Records at sector, enclave and street level are not published here. Several rest on fewer than twenty prior transactions, and two are price records where the rate per square foot actually fell against the prior high — a bigger house rather than a higher value.

Thirty pairs, and the two that lost money

All thirty matched pairs came in at exactly 0.00 per cent area variance, so every comparison in this week’s set is like-for-like. The largest gains were a detached house on Trevose Crescent, bought in October 2005 at S$5.90 million and sold at S$19.80 million, and one on Clover Avenue bought in August 2000 at S$3.50 million and sold at S$13.89 million — gains of S$13.90 million and S$10.39 million over 20.7 and 26.0 years.

The mix is broader than the top of the table suggests. Detached houses take the two largest gains and the highest median, S$7.17 million against S$2.12 million for terrace, but only four of the thirty pairs are detached. Terrace houses account for seventeen pairs and 41.6 per cent of the week’s total realised gain, against 32.1 per cent from detached and 26.3 per cent from semi-detached. Detached wins per transaction; terrace wins on volume. The median detached hold was 18.6 years against 6.9 for terrace, which explains most of the gap.

The longest hold belongs to a terrace on Happy Avenue East, bought in November 1995 for S$1.333 million and sold in July 2026 for S$4.40 million. A gain of 230 per cent over thirty years and eight months — or 3.97 per cent a year. That compounding rate, sitting behind a headline that looks spectacular, is the useful comparison for everything below.

The launch premium that never closed

The week’s clearest loss was a strata terrace on Lorong M Telok Kurau. It was bought first-hand from the developer at The Seawind in March 2013 for S$4.70 million, at S$1,404 per square foot, and sold in July 2026 for S$4.16 million at S$1,243 — a loss of S$540,000, or 11.5 per cent, over thirteen years and four months. That is minus 0.91 per cent a year.

The project’s own resale trail has risen steadily for five years: S$1,135 per square foot in 2021, S$1,144 in 2022, S$1,243 in 2025 and again in 2026. It still has not reached what the developer charged in 2013.

Strata landed — cluster housing, where the house is owned but the land is shared — has a reputation for losing money. The data says something more precise. Since 2020, strata houses have been resold at a loss about as often as ordinary landed houses: roughly one sale in thirty-five, either way. The difference is in the winners. A typical strata resale gained 31 per cent; a typical land-title resale gained 47 per cent. Strata does not lose more often. It gains less when it gains.

And the bigger divide is not strata against land title at all. It is whether the seller bought from a developer or from another owner. Buying at launch roughly halves the typical gain, whichever kind of house it is.

This week supplies a second case independently. A terrace on Bukit Way was also bought new from a developer, in September 2012 for S$3.720 million, and sold in July 2026 for S$4.300 million — 15.6 per cent over thirteen years and ten months, or 1.05 per cent a year. Two launch purchases from the same era, appearing in the same week, both at the bottom of the return table.

A short hold that is not a quick profit

A terrace on Carpmael Road shows the largest short-hold gain of the week: bought at S$5.38 million in July 2025 and sold at S$8.35 million in February 2026, 55.2 per cent in a little over six months. On the caveat record alone it would top any ranking of quick returns. It is worth setting out why it should not, because this pattern is becoming more common.

The buyer was a single-purpose company named after the address — the standard structure for a landed redevelopment — which applied to rebuild in September and sold the house with planning permission in hand. Buying a new-build from a landed developer is ordinary practice, and a brand-new house commands a substantial premium over one in original condition, more still where the existing house dates from 1953. The 56 per cent gap against another Carpmael Road terrace that transacted at S$2,319 per square foot in July 2026 is in line with what new stock fetches against old.

The point is what the data does and does not carry. The seller’s cost base includes a rebuild the caveat record never captures, and the land area reads as identical on both legs because URA records land, not what stands on it. PropertyAtlas flags holdings of three years or less for exactly this reason — the flag says be careful, it does not say why.

Data note

Source: URA REALIS caveat data, releases dated 28 and 31 July 2026. House numbers are masked on all landed transactions. Dates are caveat lodgement dates and follow the transaction, typically by one to two weeks. Profit and loss is derived by matching each sale to the most recent prior transaction at the same address, computed on transacted price before costs, taxes and financing. Where paired areas differ by more than five per cent the comparison is not like-for-like and is flagged rather than reported. Where a lease was renewed or topped up between the two transactions, the premium paid for the fresh tenure is not recorded in caveat data — the gain is therefore gross of that cost and is noted where it occurs. Holdings of three years or less may involve redevelopment, in which case the cost base is incomplete. Record comparisons are published at district level only, matched on property type and tenure, and measured against the full transaction history. This is descriptive market commentary, not investment, valuation or transactional advice.
